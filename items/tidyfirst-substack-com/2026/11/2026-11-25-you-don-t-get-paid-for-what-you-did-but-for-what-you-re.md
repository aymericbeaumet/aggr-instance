Hi, Jon! That’ll be a dollar, please.

I always have a twinge of entitlement when I see it. I created some value in the world. Some of that value should come back to me, no?

That kind of thinking—create value → get paid—violates a fundamental economic principle that I’ve been noodling on, a principle that I haven’t seen anywhere else (← so likely it’s wrong, but still worth thinking about). Here’s the principle: you don’t get paid for what you’ve done, you get paid for what you’re going to do.

Assume for the moment that an economy is a way society allocates resources to their highest & best use. (It’s also a way to create options for future highest & best uses, following Features/Futures, but that’s a thought for another day. As is the observation that economies are subject to all kinds of distortions.)

Music enriches society. Which musicians should we pay (allocate scarce music money resources to) to make music? Take the example of a one-hit wonder. They enrich society once & never will again. How much should we (society) pay them? Zero.

Yes, that strikes me as “unfair”, but we lose nothing by stiffing them. We’d rather take that same money & route it to support musicians who are going to write & perform new songs.

Not so fast. What about all the other potential one-hit wonders out there? We want their one hits in our playlist, so maybe we pay off this one-hit wonder to encourage them. We don’t want them saying, “My one hit isn’t going to net me anything so never mind.” (← interesting parallels to the wholesale ingestion of living authors’ work into LLMs.)

Another point—we can’t confidently predict that the one-hit wonder *won’t* produce a second, third, & fourth hit. So we (society) allocate money to them on the chance that they will give us another party rockin’ hit.

We don’t want to over-allocate resources to musicians who we don’t confidently expect to produce “valuable” music. That would result in fewer musicians & fewer hits. The starving artist is a social strategy to maximize the amount of art.

Take a more predictable example—auto manufacturing. You’re running a car factory. You make a car. You sell it. You’re getting paid for the car, right? Not from this perspective.

Take the last car out of the factory. Society benefits from having one more car. However, no further cars are ever going to emerge from that factory. Society should allocate zero resources to the factory. The factory doesn’t need more steel, more labor, more rubber. That last car should be free.

From this “in advance” perspective, paying for a car is allocating steel, labor, & rubber to the *next* car the factory produces.

Back to “…like Kent Beck”. Should I get paid for the value people derive from my past contributions? Only if:

- We (society) wants to incentivize others to contribute to the commons, or

- We (society) expects me to use those resources to create new value.

If someone in the next generation says, “Oh look, Kent contributed XP & TDD & JUnit & patterns & 3X & now he’s financially secure,” maybe they’re incentivized to come up with their own ideas & circulate them. Society benefits from their ideas more than the signaling cost of paying me.

From my perspective I’m not done yet. I think I have valuable contributions to make in the Genie Era but I’m about as biased as it’s possible to be. Still, I can point to coining the term “genie”, to Features Versus Futures, to individual & group coaching that folks find valuable, I can point to those things as indications that I will contribute in the future.

Next time I feel that entitlement feeling coming on, I’ll remind myself that I don’t get paid for what I’ve done, only for what I’m going to do. And get back to work.

This idea of getting paid in advance struck me in 2003 while I was sitting alone on a chair lift. I’ve tried many times to write it up but always come up short. It wasn’t until it became personal that I had enough incentive to get through this essay. I’m not an economist. What are people going to say? Blah blah blah. Here it is. I hope you find it helpful.
