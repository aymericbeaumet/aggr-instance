While there’s been an enormous outpouring about the drama of this situation, so far it’s hard to find good simple accounts of Masullo’s paper. Let me try to fill this gap by sketching Masullo’s proof strategy in a very simplified way, along with some problems people have noted.

This implies many results about primes in arithmetic progressions. For example, Hardy and Littlewood showed it implies that primes equal to 3 mod 4 are more common than primes 1 mod 4. They also showed it implies every odd number > 5 is a sum of three primes! (Helfgott found an alternative, more complicated proof in 2013.)

But Masullo also wants more: he wants to prove the analogous statement for L-functions of general Hecke characters. These involve a choice of algebraic number field , which gives a ring of integers generalizing the usual integers. This further generalization implies a huge number of results in number theory, computational complexity, and so on.

The left side is the sum of the eigenvalues of This is the ‘spectral side’ of the trace formula. The right side is the sum of the diagonal entries, the ‘geometric side’. They are equal, even though they are computed in different ways.

For operators on infinite-dimensional spaces the same idea works, with care. The trace of an integral operator with kernel is the integral of over the diagonal. In the famous Selberg trace formula, the spectral side involves eigenvalues of the Laplacian on a hyperbolic surface, while the geometric side involves the lengths of closed geodesics. Believe it or not, closed geodesics behave a lot like primes! This analogy has inspired many attempts on the Riemann Hypothesis.

In 1952, André Weil reformulated the explicit formula for primes and zeros in this spirit. He also showed that the generalized Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to a certain functional, built from the explicit formula, being positive. This is called Weil’s positivity criterion.

In the late 1990s, Alain Connes went further. He found an operator on a space built from the adeles of a number field whose trace formula is the explicit formula. In his words,

This reduces the Riemann hypothesis to the validity of the trace formula….

Masullo therefore approximates it by smooth, compactly supported functions, built from a cutoff that increases to the characteristic function of He then takes a limit. To pass to the limit, he needs the approximating expressions to be uniformly bounded on compact sets. Then a standard compactness theorem for holomorphic functions, Montel's theorem, gives a subsequence converging to a holomorphic limit.

So the argument seems to have two hard parts. First, the trace formula itself, with Masullo’s new computation of the geometric side: this is what most of the 940 pages are devoted to. Second, the final limiting step: does the geometric side really stay under control as the cutoff approaches the jump at If either part fails, the proof fails.

Some possible problems

Vakil asked people to check Masullo’s argument. They did, and within hours problems were reported:

A problem at the prime 2. An AI-assisted review organized by Eric Naslund tests one of the manuscript's theorems on a test function concentrated near the number 2. The theorem predicts the answer log 2. But the review argues that the limit used to compute it involves two conditions that no point can satisfy simultaneously, so the limit is 0.

A functional analysis step. Participants in an online checking effort claim to have spotted other mistakes. For example, on page 444 Masullo extends some terminology for bases from the setting of ‘Schauder bases’, where the coordinate projections are uniformly bounded, to the more general ‘Markushevich bases’, where they need not be. He calls a basis ‘regular’ if its vectors stay a fixed distance away from zero, and adds that “in particular” the functionals extracting coordinates are then bounded. For Schauder bases this is true. For Markushevich bases it’s apparently false. Supposedly this false claim is used later in the manuscript. Maybe the specific bases Masullo uses have the needed bound for other reasons.

These objections may turn out to be fixable or mistaken. And remember, the whole paper is openly a draft. For example, Chapter 13, where the generalized Riemann hypothesis should finally be deduced, contains only placeholders where the theorem should be! Brian Conrad, who has been reading the manuscript for months, told Scientific American it is undergoing major revision. The original plan was for Masullo to spend a year polishing the text with Conrad before any announcement. Alpöge's claim forced a hasty release.

Why it’s interesting anyway

Even if Masullo's argument fails, it's worth understanding. It tries to carry out a well-known strategy: realize the zeros as the spectrum of an operator, realize the primes as the "closed orbits" on the other side of a trace formula, and read off the generalized Riemann hypothesis from that duality. It is quite unlike Alpöge's argument, which transplants Stepanov’s proof from finite fields to number fields and uses heavy machinery from automorphic forms. So even if one of these proofs turns out to be right, it won't make the other one redundant.

And if Masullo's argument can't be saved, we will learn exactly where this particular version of the trace-formula dream breaks down. That is what happens to most attempts on the Riemann Hypothesis. This one gets to happen in the glare of public view, at high speed.
