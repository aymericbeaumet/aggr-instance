As corporate earnings soar and U.S. GDP balloons, the American workforce isn’t feeling the same boom. American workers are taking home less of the country’s overall income, data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics shows, and employment in the U.S. is set to continue to slow.

Labor share, or the portion of the U.S.’s economic output that workers receive through salary and wages, decreased to 52.8% in the second quarter of 2026, its lowest level since the BLS started recording this data in 1947, according to its [labor productivity and costs report](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm). In the previous quarter, labor share was at 53.6%. This decade, the labor share average was about 55.6%.

That’s despite corporate earnings skyrocketing, with profits for Fortune 500 companies [hitting a record $2.1 trillion](https://fortune.com/2026/06/19/fortune-500-employment-profits-revenue-hiring-jobs-ai/) in 2025. The [U.S. GDP grew 3.4%](https://www.bea.gov/data/gdp/gross-domestic-product) in the third quarter last year, exceeding economists’ predictions.

That growth has not only come at the expense of how much of the pie of wealth workers are taking home, but also how many Americans are in the workforce, economists warn.

“That decline in the share of labor has got to be either falling earnings or falling numbers of people,” Raymond Robertson, a labor economist at Texas A&M’s Bush School of Government, told *Fortune.* “The falling share of income is having to do with the shift towards capital.”

Indeed, there are growing signs that as national income balloons, the U.S. workforce is deflating. Unemployment [ticked up to 4.2%](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm) in September, and employers added just 584,000 jobs in 2025 compared to 2 million added in 2024. The U.S. added 29,000 jobs in September, about one-third of economists’ projections.

The bifurcation of corporate victories and weak labor data raises concerns among economists of [jobless growth](https://fortune.com/2026/01/07/jobless-expansion-low-hire-fire-economy-k-shaped-december-november-data-jolts-reaction/) jeopardizing the U.S. workforce, as well as a [K-shaped economy](https://fortune.com/2025/11/07/what-is-the-k-shaped-economy-wealth-inequality-explainer/), where the rich get richer while the poor get poorer, becoming more exaggerated.

“Data right now is very mixed,” Robertson said. “But I think it also all consistently points to this idea that things are getting worse for workers and much better for billionaires.”

## **What is causing jobless growth?**

Robertson attributes weakening labor share averages to the rise in automation, which he noted is displacing workers, with productivity—a metric essentially measuring worker output—continuing to rise. Second-quarter GDP data showed nonfarm productivity growth [grew 1.4% last quarter](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/prod2.nr0.htm), though the rate has slowed from 2.2% in the same quarter a year ago.

“All these things, bit by bit, are replacing people, and they’re concentrating income and their share of capital,” he said.

[Goldman Sachs](https://fortune.com/company/goldman-sachs-group/) analysts [Joseph Briggs](http://linkedin.com/in/joseph-briggs-997140100) and [Sarah Dong](https://www.linkedin.com/in/sarahfdong/) estimated in a report, based on Department of Labor job numbers, that AI automation could [displace 25% of all work hours](https://fortune.com/2026/01/13/humans-could-go-the-way-of-horses-goldman-ai-job-apocalypse-unemployment/). They predicted that over the course of the AI adoption period, a 15% increase in AI-driven productivity would displace 6% to 7% of jobs, and, at its peak, a 1 million increase in unemployed workers, though job displacement as a result of AI has [not shown up meaningfully](https://fortune.com/2026/02/02/ai-labor-market-yale-budget-lab-ai-washing/) in any economic data.

The displacement could be substantial, the analysts said, but said the impacts of automation will be tempered by a wealth of new jobs created as a result of the technological changes.

Automation is expected to be a boon to corporate profits and GDP, projected to boost GDP by 1.5% by 2035, according to a [Wharton brief](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/issues/2025/9/8/projected-impact-of-generative-ai-on-future-productivity-growth) published in September 2025. Early signs indicate AI could drive productivity gains, with around 80% of employees reporting AI makes them faster at specific tasks, but only 37% of companies seeing an impact of that productivity on operating profits, according to [McKinsey data](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai), and AI operating costs continue to constrain its deployment.

Robertson added that growing unemployment, which he expects to see rise over the next few months, keeps wages down, allowing margins and profits to expand.

To be sure, the recent productivity surge has been an “[open question](https://fortune.com/2026/01/12/why-is-productivity-up-4-9-percent-gdp-growth-jobs-ai-layoffs/),” [Morgan Stanley](https://fortune.com/company/morgan-stanley/) economists have argued, not unanimously attributed to increased adoption of AI or automation. The analysts suggested this increase would be cyclical, or vestiges of pandemic-era habits of companies making more from less.

An [Oxford Economists research brief](https://fortune.com/2026/01/07/ai-layoffs-convenient-corporate-fiction-true-false-oxford-economics-productivity/) from earlier this year suggested companies are disguising overhiring-related layoffs as a result of AI, but said automation-related workforce reductions have not yet happened en masse. Additionally, while unemployment has been ticking up over the past year, it is still relatively low.

## **Why is an immigration crackdown backfiring on U.S. labor**?

Mark Regets, senior fellow at National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP), sees a different reason for a slowing workforce. He told *Fortune* President Donald Trump’s [immigration crackdown](https://fortune.com/2025/10/21/trump-immigration-policy-jobs-workforce-american-workers-gdp-growth/) has not done what Trump administration officials, such as White House Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller, said it would in [increasing the number of U.S.-born workers](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/11/11/us/politics/trump-2025-immigration-agenda.html). Instead, according to Regets, Trump’s immigration policies have not only decimated the foreign-born workforce, but has also created fewer opportunities for domestic-born workers to find jobs.

NFAP data shows a [1.1 million to 1.4 million-person reduction](https://nfap.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/US-Labor-Force-Analysis-Jan-2025-to-June-2026.NFAP-Policy-Brief.July-2026.pdf) in the foreign-born workforce since January 2025, consistent with the [Congressional Budget Office’s report last year](https://fortune.com/2025/09/11/congressional-budget-office-trump-immigration-population-growth-inflation-gdp/) indicating shrinking U.S. population growth as a result of migrants being deported or refusing to come to the U.S. out of fear of hostile polities.

“The data is raising huge red flags that we are losing immigrants of all types that we otherwise would be advancing America’s economy,” Regets said.

Recent [BLS data](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.t07.htm) is counter-evidence to Miller’s argument that harsher immigration policy would grow the U.S. workforce. The labor participation rate for foreign-born workers fell from 66.3% to 66% from September 2025 to September 2026, and labor participation for native-born workers has similarly fallen from 61.6% to 60.9% over the same period. Fewer immigrant workers may actually make it harder for U.S.-born individuals to find work.

“A company unable to find the workers it needs for some roles could shut down operations rather than continuing,” Regets said.

He noted skillset diversity in a workplace could boost productivity and justify employing more people. Greater immigration can also increase consumer spending and stimulate businesses, as well as encourage businesses to take advantage of ample labor market availability and seek out their labor instead of offshoring jobs.

## **How does the U.S. reverse a shrinking labor force**?

While friendlier immigration policies could help reverse an exodus of foreign-born workers, Robertson said addressing the workplace automation push would be key to growing the U.S. workforce.

“There are trades that are technology-assisted,” he said. “Those are going to be in higher demand, but you really still have to have a significant investment in skills.”

The young generation of workers are already prepared to adapt to a changing labor landscape. Gen Z are [flocking to trade schools](https://fortune.com/article/trade-school-vs-college-parents-job-market/) in hopes of a finding a job as a carpenter or welder not so easily outsourced by AI, and in 2024, enrollment in vocation-based community colleges [increased 16%](https://fortune.com/2024/04/04/gen-z-choosing-trade-schools-college-welders-carpenters-six-figure-job/), according to data from the National Student Clearinghouse.

Companies have taken it upon themselves to [provide reskilling opportunities](https://fortune.com/2026/10/11/mckinsey-research-11-million-americans-new-job-next-decade/) to employees. A CareerTrainer [survey](https://careertrainer.ai/en/reports/ai-upskilling-statistics/) from June found that 72% of hiring managers intended to reskill employees. While the U.S. Department of Labor [updated guidelines](http://r) to encourage states to adapt workplace development systems, Robertson argued the government hasn’t done enough in several decades to imbue the workforce with necessary skillsets for future jobs.

“Democrats and Republicans have not significantly invested in training \[or\] the retraining or active labor market programs that you need to match workers to jobs,” Robertson said. “That’s the obvious solution.”

Without changes, economists see the pattern of an employment slowdown continuing, but with greater concern about the ability for the U.S. economy to sustain growth.

“We need job growth to have a growing economy, and I think we need job growth to pay our debts,” Regets said. “I don’t know how you have job growth with a shrinking labor force.”
