I started building restorekit.org dongles a few months ago at the Recurse Center. They are small inline USB-C boards that sit between a host computer and a target mac. They set the target mac into DFUDevice Firmware Update: Apple's lowest-level recovery mode built into the boot ROM, so even a bricked Mac can be reflashed. mode over USB-PDUSB Power Delivery: the communication protocol over the CC wire used to negotiate power, roles, and vendor-defined commands. without having to touch the target at all, and work with an open-source toolkit for restoring macs without the need for another mac.

The original board, the Dongle-Lite, is USB 2 only. A single USB 2 hub (CH334F) connects both the dongle’s MCU and the target mac through a single cable, while a PD PHYPhysical-layer transceiver chip that encodes and decodes USB Power Delivery packets on the CC wire. (FUSB302B) sends Apple’s DFU commands on the CC line.

I recently learned from timo at Twocanoes that newer Macs support USB 3 speeds for recovery. I obviously immediately wanted to support this on the hub for faster recovery, but the problem was cost. At first I designed a “proper” USB 3 board using a GL3510 USB 3 hub and a pair of TI SuperSpeed muxes (HD3SS3220 and HD3SS3212). It basically doubled the parts count, and BOMBill of Materials: the full list of components and quantities required to assemble one board. cost, required me to increase the size of PCB to ~100 mm, and topped out at 5 Gbps transfers between host and target (USB 3.1 Gen 1).

I really didn’t want to make a board that cost more than twice the Dongle-Lite just to get USB 3 speeds.

So with the 2nd version Dongle-Pro, I tried a kinda crazy USB-C experiment: drop the USB 3 hub completely, keep a cheap USB 2 hub on D+/D-, and route the USB 3 SuperSpeedUSB 3.x's dedicated high-speed TX/RX differential pairs, physically separate from USB 2's D+/D- wires. lanes as a straight passthrough from host to target on the same USB-C port. The idea being that in theory the USB 3 and 2 controllers on a USB-C are completely independent, and should work simultaneously.

Since high-speed USB 3 is super prone to interference I didn’t really have a choice other than to purchase a view boards and test it out.

Well… I just got the boards in from JLC and… THEY WORK! They establish a full 10 Gbps USB 3.2 Gen 2 connection while connecting the dongle’s USB 2 hub and MCU over the exact same port. After some aggressive cost optimization, the 10 Gbps board also ended up cheaper and smaller than the USB 2 Dongle-Lite.

Like I mentioned earlier, USB-C is not a single data bus. Inside a standard USB-C port, USB 2 and USB 3 sit on completely different sets of pins:

USB-C 24-Pin Spec

Every USB-C port has two mirrored 12-pin rows: A1 through A12 on top, and B12 through B1 on the bottom, so a cable works rotated either way.

USB 3 SuperSpeedUSB 2 (D+/D-)CC / USB-PDSBU (Apple UART)Power / GND

  • USB 2 (D+ / D-): a single 480 Mbps pair in the center (pins A6/A7 and B6/B7 are shorted together).
  • USB 3 (TX/RX): 2 separate high-speed lanes on the outer edge. Only 1 set is active depending on orientation.
  • Control & sideband (CC, SBU): CC1/CC2 detect cable orientation and negotiate USB-PD; SBU1/SBU2 (A8/B8) are used by Apple’s 1.2 V debug UART.

This continues to the computer’s xHCIExtensible Host Controller Interface: the USB controller inside your computer that manages both USB 2 and USB 3.x root ports. controller: a single physical USB-C port is actually wired to two independent root ports: a USB 2 port and a USB 3 port.

A standard USB 3 hub chip like the GL3510 is actually just two hubs in 1 package (a USB 2 hub and a USB 3 hub). On my first iteration of the Dongle-Pro, everything ran through that chip even though the dongle’s own MCU only speaks USB 2 Full Speed (12 Mbps) and has no need for the USB 3 speed.

On the Dongle-Pro v2, the two buses are split apart:

  • USB 2 (D+/D-) runs into a CH334P USB 2 hub. One downstream port goes to the target’s D+/D- (so DFU mode and any USB 2 device behave just like the Lite), and another goes to the dongle’s MCU (CH32X035G8U6).
  • USB 3 (TX/RX) skips the hub completely. Each port’s two lane sets go into a CH9441P 2:1 muxMultiplexer: a high-speed analog switch that routes whichever side of a reversible USB-C plug is active onto one pair of board traces.. Each mux reads its own port’s CC1/CC2 lines to handle cable flip and connects the active lane set to its common side. The two muxes sit in the center of the board, wired straight together through a short corridor with no hub and no redriverAn active signal-conditioning chip that boosts and equalizes high-speed USB 3 traces to overcome cable and PCB insertion loss..

What Could Go Wrong

This sounded like it would work to me on paper, but there were a few things I thought could go wrong:

  1. Two passive muxes in series with no redriver. Every connector, ESD array, via, and switch adds insertion loss and reflections. I was hoping two CH9441P chips back-to-back would pass 5 Gbps. But hitting 10 Gbps is even better.
  2. Sharing the target CC lines. On the target port, the HUSB311 PD PHY actively drives the CC lines to send Apple’s SOP” DebugA USB-PD packet framing addressed to a debug accessory rather than a normal device, required for Apple's DFU commands. VDMsVendor-Defined Messages: custom USB-PD packets. Apple uses them to trigger DFU, reboot, and serial debug without touching the keyboard.. The target’s CH9441P is also reading the lines to steer its lanes.
  3. Host OS behavior. Even though it made sense in theory, I really wasn’t sure if a machine would happily enumerate a USB 2 hub on a port’s D+/D- lines while a separate USB 3.2 Gen 2 device negotiated directly with the same port’s SuperSpeed lanes.

Companion Ports

Splitting the bus required one interesting change in the host SDK (restorekit).

On the Dongle-Lite, whatever device you plugged into the dongle always showed up as a child of the dongle’s hub. That made it easy to tell which target Mac belonged to which dongle when you plugged in several at once.

On the Dongle-Pro v2, that breaks whenever the target connects at USB 3. Just like iPhone@01200000 above, a SuperSpeed target bypasses the CH334P hub completely and shows up as a sibling directly on the host’s root hub.

Turns out the OS already tracks which USB 2 and USB 3 root ports share the same physical port and it exposes it directly:

  • On macOS, the USB 2 port’s IORegistrymacOS's live kernel tree of hardware devices, ports, and drivers, which you can inspect with ioreg. entry has a UsbProtocolCompanion (3.x) property containing the path to its SuperSpeed twin.
  • On Linux, the USB 2 port’s sysfsLinux's virtual filesystem at /sys that exposes kernel devices, USB hubs, and physical port peer links. directory has a port/peer symlink pointing straight to the SuperSpeed port.

So restorekit can still know with 100% certainty which 10 Gbps device is plugged into which dongle.

Cutting the Cost

I originally planned to have a Dongle-Lite and significantly more expensive USB 3 Pro, but that means having to maintain two hardware lines, so if I could cut the cost of the USB 3 version to be comparably priced to the original Dongle-Lite it felt like a no brainer.

After I removed the GL3510 USB 3 hub from the original design, I went through the rest of the board to see what else I could cut:

  • SuperSpeed path: HD3SS3220 ($1.66) + HD3SS3212 ($1.55) + GL3510 ($1.23) came to $4.44 on Pro v1 for 5 Gbps. Two CH9441P muxes cost $0.47 total ($0.24 each) on Pro v2 for 10 Gbps. Pro v1’s SuperSpeed chips alone cost more than the entire Pro v2 BOM.
  • MCU: Swapped the RP2354A + 3.3 uH inductor + crystal ($1.62) with a CH32X035G8U6 ($0.42), running off its internal 48 MHz HSIHigh-Speed Internal oscillator: an on-chip clock source that lets an MCU run without an external quartz crystal. with no crystal, no external inductor, and a built-in ROM USB bootloader.
  • PD PHY: Swapped the FUSB302BMPX ($0.68) with a HUSB311BLA ($0.51).
  • USB 2 hub: Swapped the CH334F ($0.53) with a CH334P ($0.31), which integrates the crystal load capacitors and drops two more passives.
  • 1.2 V SBU UART level shifters: two 74AVC1T45 translators plus a dedicated TLV70212 1.2 V LDO ($0.79) became two HL2302 MOSFETs and a few resistors ($0.05) wired as common-gate level shifters.
  • Unified ESD and passive discipline: swapped mixed USBLC6 + TPD4E05U06 parts for six H5VU25U flow-through arrays (JLCPCB Preferred Extended = $0 feeder fee), and limited the whole board to just three resistor values and three capacitor values.

These swaps resulted in a 72% BOM reduction from original Dongle-Pro while doubling the SuperSpeed link rate from 5 Gbps to 10 Gbps, and a 42% BOM reduction compared to the Dongle-Lite.

Conclusion

If you are building an inline USB-C tool where your MCU only needs a slow control channel and the host just needs fast passthrough to the downstream device, you might not need a USB 3 hub. USB-C gives you two independent buses in the same port.
