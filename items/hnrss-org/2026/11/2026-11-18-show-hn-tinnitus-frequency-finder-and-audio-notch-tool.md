## What this is, and who it's for

Tinnitus is a sound you hear that isn't coming from outside: a ringing, hiss, whistle or buzz that only you can hear. It's common, and it's hard to describe to anyone else.

This tool helps you put a number on its pitch. You compare a few sounds with the one you hear and get a frequency in hertz, with a range.

### What people do with the number

- Tell an audiologist or doctor something more exact than "a high ringing"
- Play the pitch to someone else, so they can hear what you hear
- Play background sound with that pitch notched out, in [the free Notched player](https://trynotched.com/tools.html) or the [Mac app](https://trynotched.com/index.html)
- Match again later and see whether it has changed

Knowing the number doesn't change your tinnitus, and this isn't a hearing test or a diagnosis.

## Why this is more than a tone slider

### It sets levels for your ears

Most people hear high pitches less well, so one fixed volume makes high sounds seem fainter and skews the match. A short level check makes every test sound about equally easy to hear, and finds where your hearing or headphones stop.

### It checks the octave

The most common mistake in pitch matching is landing exactly one octave off. After you narrow down, you compare your match with the sounds an octave above and below.

### It gives a range, not a fake decimal

You match twice, by two different methods, and get a range that allows for day-to-day variation. Match again on other days and the page averages them, and tells you when they agree. ### Before you start

Use headphones if you can, wired if you have them. Turn off spatial audio and any equalizer. Sit somewhere quiet. Every sound starts very quiet and you can stop it at any time.

### See a doctor if

Your hearing dropped suddenly, the sound is in one ear only, it pulses with your heartbeat, or it comes with dizziness or ear pain. This tool cannot tell you why you hear a sound.

## How the match works, step by step

1. **Safety check**You tick any warning signs, such as sudden hearing loss or a sound that pulses with your heartbeat. If any apply, the page tells you to see a doctor first.
2. **Describe your sound**You say whether it is a tone, a hiss or several sounds, and where you hear it. A tone or several sounds are matched with pure tones, a hiss or buzz with bands of noise.
3. **Set your volume**One quiet reference sound lets you set your device volume, which you then leave alone.
4. **Level check**Nine sounds from 1,000 to 14,000 Hz each rise from silence, and you tap when you can just hear each one. Every later test sound then plays a fixed step above that level, so high pitches are as easy to hear as low ones. This also finds where your hearing or headphones stop.
5. **Explore**Up to eleven sounds from 750 to 14,000 Hz play in random order, and you say whether each is nowhere near your sound, getting warm, or very close. This is a rough sort; the next step fine-tunes it.
6. **Narrow down, then check the octave**Six pairs of sounds close in on your pitch by forced choice: you pick the closer of two, and the gap shrinks from half an octave to about a twelfth. You then compare your match with the sounds one octave above and below, which catches the most common error in pitch matching.
7. **Match by hand**A second, independent match: you slide a quiet, continuous tone up from a starting point below your first match until it matches. The slider reaches 16,000 Hz, and if your sound is higher still you can say so. It fades in, and you can turn it down at any time. If your two matches land far apart, you hear both and choose the closer one.

The result is the middle of your two matches, shown in hertz with a range and the nearest musical note. Results are kept on your device and averaged when you match again on other days.

## Common questions

What is tinnitus, and who is this tool for?

Tinnitus is a sound you hear that isn't coming from outside: a ringing, hiss, whistle or buzz that only you can hear. This tool is for people who have it and want to put a number on its pitch. If you don't hear a sound like that, there is nothing to match, though you can still listen to the [single tones](https://trynotched.com/tones/) to hear what different frequencies sound like.

Why would I want to know my tinnitus frequency?

Mostly because it makes the sound something you can describe. "A ringing around 6,000 Hz" is easier to tell a doctor, an audiologist or a partner than "a high noise". It also lets you use sound tools that ask for a frequency, lets you play the pitch to someone else so they can hear what you hear, and gives you a way to notice if it changes over time. Knowing the number does not change the tinnitus itself.

How do I find my tinnitus frequency?

Compare outside sounds with the sound you hear until one matches in pitch. This tool guides that in about four minutes: it sets levels for your hearing, has you rate and compare sounds to narrow down, checks for an octave error, and then has you match a second time by hand. You get a frequency in hertz with a range.

How accurate is an online tinnitus frequency test?

A single pitch match is approximate, whoever runs it. People often land a few semitones apart on different days, and octave errors are common. That is why this tool reports a range instead of one exact number, and averages your matches across days. Treat one result as a good estimate, and repeat it on two or three other days.

What is octave confusion?

Octave confusion is matching a sound exactly one octave above or below the true pitch, which is half or double the frequency. Tones an octave apart sound alike, so it is the most common mistake in pitch matching. This tool checks for it by asking you to compare your match with the sounds an octave above and below.

Do I need headphones?

Headphones are strongly recommended, wired if possible. Laptop and phone speakers are weak at high frequencies, where most tinnitus pitches sit. Turn off spatial audio and any equalizer first.

What frequency is tinnitus usually?

It varies from person to person. Matches most often fall between about 3,000 and 8,000 Hz, usually near a range where hearing is reduced, but they can be lower or higher. This tool tests from 500 to 14,000 Hz.

Is this a hearing test or a diagnosis?

No. It matches the pitch of a sound you already hear. It does not measure hearing loss and cannot tell you why you hear a sound. If your hearing dropped suddenly, the sound is in one ear only, it pulses with your heartbeat, or it comes with dizziness or ear pain, see a doctor promptly.

Is it free, and is my data private?

It is free with no sign-up. Everything runs in your browser. Your results are saved only on your own device so the page can average them across days.

What can I do with my result?

Write it down, or use Copy result, and take it to an audiologist appointment as a starting point. Play it back on the [single-tone pages](https://trynotched.com/tones/) to check it, or to let someone else hear it. Match again on two other days so the page can average them. If you like the idea of listening with that pitch filtered out, the free [Notched phone app](https://trynotched.com/tools.html) plays background sound with a gap at your pitch, [this page](https://trynotched.com/listen.html) does the same to a song of your own, and the Mac app does it to everything your Mac plays.
