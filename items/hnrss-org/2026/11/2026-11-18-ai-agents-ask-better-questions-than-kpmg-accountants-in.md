**LONDON, November 18, 2026** — Artificial intelligence has taken another significant step into the world of professional services after KPMG revealed that AI agents examining the accounts of a major UK-listed company sometimes investigated financial issues more deeply and asked better questions than its human audit team.

The experiment, conducted by KPMG UK using more than 100 AI agents, marks an important development in the application of artificial intelligence to one of the most labour-intensive areas of the financial services industry. It also raises an uncomfortable question for the accountancy profession: if machines can increasingly perform the analytical work traditionally undertaken by highly trained professionals, how quickly will the economics of auditing change?

According to the *Financial Times*, the project, codenamed Project Exponential, examined the financial statements of an unnamed FTSE 250 company. The AI agents worked alongside a conventional audit team, undertaking tasks across the audit process rather than simply assisting with isolated administrative functions.

KPMG UK head of audit Cath Burnet said the technology had, in several instances, gone considerably deeper into issues than the regular audit team, identifying areas requiring further investigation and asking questions that human auditors had not necessarily raised.

The findings suggest that AI’s potential in professional services may extend well beyond improving productivity. It could fundamentally change how complex financial information is examined, interpreted and challenged.

## From supporting accountants to conducting audits

Project Exponential represents a shift from using AI as a supporting tool towards deploying autonomous agents across an entire audit workflow.

The agents collected information, analysed and documented data, developed draft conclusions and observations, and suggested subsequent steps. They were also able to work alongside one another, effectively dividing responsibilities and coordinating elements of the investigation.

The technology was developed over approximately four months. However, KPMG was careful to distinguish the experiment from a formally approved statutory audit. Although an audit partner signed off on the experimental exercise, its findings were not used as the basis for formally approving the company’s accounts.

Human oversight remained essential. During the trial, an auditor had to intervene when one AI agent failed to correctly match regulatory requirements with the company’s documentation. The episode illustrates both the capabilities and limitations of current AI systems when dealing with complex regulatory frameworks.

KPMG plans to expand the initiative to as many as 20 larger audits, although the firm acknowledges that the technology is not yet sufficiently mature to be deployed across thousands of engagements. Data quality also remains a significant obstacle, with some businesses lacking the clean, structured information required for AI agents to operate effectively.

## A challenge to the Big Four business model?

The implications for the accountancy industry could be substantial.

Traditional auditing relies on large teams of junior accountants undertaking detailed and repetitive work, supervised by experienced managers and partners. AI agents potentially offer a different operating model, automating significant portions of data collection, analysis and documentation while allowing senior professionals to concentrate on complex judgements and exceptions.

For firms such as KPMG, Deloitte, PwC and EY, the attraction is clear: faster audits, potentially lower operating costs and the ability to analyse much larger volumes of financial information.

However, whether those savings will ultimately be passed on to clients remains uncertain. KPMG has acknowledged the potential for significant time savings but has not confirmed whether AI-driven efficiencies will translate into lower audit fees.

## Human judgement remains the final safeguard

The transition also presents regulatory and professional challenges. The UK’s Financial Reporting Council has warned that the use of AI must not undermine auditor independence, integrity or professional scepticism.

There are also concerns about data security, over-reliance on automated conclusions and the possibility of AI generating inaccurate or fabricated information. The fundamental responsibility for an audit opinion remains with the human auditor, regardless of the technology used.

The KPMG experiment does not establish that AI can replace accountants or independently deliver a reliable statutory audit. It does, however, demonstrate that AI agents are beginning to challenge professionals in areas that were once considered dependent on human expertise.

For the accountancy profession, the question is no longer simply whether AI will become part of the audit process. It is how much of that process will eventually be performed by machines — and what role human accountants will play when it does.
