Joe Stiglitz: “Economists spend enormous energy providing refined testing to their models. Economists often seem to forget that some of the most important theories in physics are either verified or refuted by a single observation, or a limited number of observations (e.g. Einstein’s theory of relativity, or the theory of black holes).”

Can we condense down the most important lessons we’ve learned from macro and macrohistory to a minimal set of facts? Here is one attempt: macrohistory in six (plus or minus) facts.


FACT 0: GDP per person correlates with good things

What I have here on the X-axis is GDP per person, adjusted for inflation and cost of living differences. On the Y-axis is life satisfaction, based on going around and asking people, ‘on a scale of zero to ten, how satisfied are you with your life’.

In richer countries, on average people are happier.

Or – in richer countries, on average people live longer:

Or – in richer countries, fewer babies die:

Are all these good things just coming from people working more? No – in richer countries, people work less; and as countries become richer over time, people work less:

More intangible things, too. You can ask people how much they trust their neighbors. In richer countries, people trust their neighbors more:

Of course, we know market capitalism has its flaws – for example, richer countries emit more CO2 per person:

And all of this is correlation not causation.

But we’re going to run with this, and use GDP per person as our measure of how well we are doing.

Which brings us to:


FACT 1: Everyone used to be poor

This is the most important fact about human history.

We are ∼10 times richer than our great-great-great grandparents, who themselves in turn were ∼2 times as rich as Jesus of Nazareth.

Everyone used to be poor. This is the most important fact about macro history.

Why did we used to be poor? We lived in a Malthusian hellscape:


FACT 1b: Wages were stagnant before the Industrial Revolution

This shows the real wage of workers in England since 1250.

Prior to the Industrial Revolution, any time wages rose, more babies would survive to adulthood, parents would have more babies, and the population would rise until wages were driven back to subsistence.

We lived in a Malthusian hellscape. Until:


FACT 2: Growth accelerated with the Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution was the single most important event in human history.

We went from 0.01% growth per year, up to 0.1% with the Industrial Revolution around 1750, to 1% with the Second Industrial Revolution around 1870, to the modern 2% growth rate.

The Industrial Revolution was the single most important event in macro history. It also brought about:


FACT 2b: The Great Divergence

The growth explosion from the Industrial Revolution did not take place everywhere and all at once. Many countries did not see faster growth and rising GDP per person until much later – and many still have not seen these much at all.

But let’s talk first about the frontier.


FACT 3: Growth at the frontier has been ∼constant in the modern era

This is the most important fact in modern macro. Growth at the technological frontier – measured here as GDP per person in the United States – has been a shockingly steady 2% per year for the last 150 years.

You can see a temporary blip down for the Great Depression, but otherwise the trend has been remarkably constant.

Running water, electricity, automobiles, computers, the web – all show up here as a continuation of a steady 2% per year trend.

What about the rest of the world?


FACT 4: Income varies enormously across countries

The figure here has on the X-axis, income per person per day. You can see, for example, that even the poorest 11% of people in the United States have on average a higher income than the median person in Bangladesh or Ethiopia.

Income varies enormously across countries. Those of us born in the United States are so, so lucky.

Well, are we stuck with these enormous differences? No:


FACT 5: There are growth miracles – and growth disasters

Here again we see the United States up top as the frontier.

Japan and South Korea, at the turn of the 20th century, were ∼as poor as sub-Saharan Africa is today. But Japan and South Korea caught up close to the United States. There are growth miracles.

But there are also growth disasters. Argentina at the turn of the 20th century was one of the richest countries in the world. But it has stagnated, relative to the frontier. A classic basket case.

And there are countries like Nigeria, which have never experienced much sustained growth at all.

What should we expect of the future? This takes us to the most fun fact, the most speculative:


FACT 6: Aggregate growth is speeding up

Over the long sweep of history, aggregate GDP growth is speeding up.

What I have plotted here is how long it takes for world GDP to double. This goes back to 1 million years ago. Measuring GDP in 1 million BC is hard, but for our purposes I feel good about these numbers.

We’ve gone from doubling every 100,000 years down to a modern doubling time of every 35 or so years.

———

Now, to the natural question: what should we expect about growth in the future?

I think it really comes down to: which prior do you want to anchor to?

Do you want to anchor to the last 150 years or so, with our constant 2% growth and doubling every 35 years?

Or do you want to anchor to the long sweep of macrohistory, where growth has been speeding up?


Summing up

We live in a weird time.

Walking around outside, I think we should really feel bombarded with this fact, every day. All these buildings? All these people? All this stuff?

This is all kind of crazy. It’s all a little bonkers. Compared to the grand sweep of macrohistory, we live in a weird time.


Meta comments

I found these “Six Big Facts of Macrohistory” particularly useful as framing when teaching my undergrad macro course for the first time last fall: teaching GDP construction (fact 0), the Malthusian model (fact 1), the theories of the Industrial Revolution (fact 2), the Solow model (facts 3-5), and the endogenous growth model (fact 6).

It was pedagogically very useful to have this catechism of the Six Facts to review at the start of many classes, to anchor the students to where we were in the course.

It was also useful for me, for setting a target for the bare minimum that students might remember in a decade. Do I expect them to remember the capital accumulation equation of the Solow model? No. Or even the idea of income vs. substitution effects? For the vast majority – no, not really.

Can I hope that they pick up a gut-level sense, in their bones, that everyone used to be poor? That’s an extremely important idea, a realistic goal, and yet an idea that the vast majority of people do not know or have not properly internalized.

I also think the Six Big Facts of Macrohistory is a useful attempt to condense “what should any well-informed civilian know about macro and macrohistory”. E.g. my microeconomist wife, or my parents.

———

What are the runner-up facts not listed here? Structural transformation and urbanization? The demographic transition? Women’s emancipation or rising health would be the natural top candidates, but are captured (if perhaps buried) in Fact 0.
