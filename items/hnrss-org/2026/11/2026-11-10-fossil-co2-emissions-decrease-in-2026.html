Global carbon emissions from fossil fuels are projected to decrease by 0.4% in 2026 – driven by expansion of solar and wind power and the Iran conflict – according to new research by the Global Carbon Project.

The 2026 Global Carbon Budget projects 38.2 billion tonnes of fossil carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions this year.

Emissions from oil are projected to decrease by 1.9% – likely a short-term response to the energy shock triggered by the Iran conflict.

While global energy demand continues to rise, two thirds of the increase is being met by increased solar and wind power and electric vehicles.

However, the report – published alongside a new paper in the journal Nature Climate Change – finds that clean energy growth is not yet fast enough for a durable decline in global fossil CO2 emissions.

With emissions from land-use change (such as deforestation) projected to increase slightly this year, total CO2 emissions are projected to be 43.3 billion tonnes in 2026 – up from 42.8 billion tonnes last year.

The remaining carbon budget (the amount of CO2 that can be emitted before warming reaches 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels) is now 150 billion tonnes – equivalent to less than four years of emissions at the current rate.

With CO2 still rapidly accumulating in the atmosphere, the dangerous impacts of climate change continue to increase.

The report also assesses natural “sinks” (absorption of CO2 by land and ocean). It finds that the land sink returned to former levels after a severe weakening during the 2023-24 El Niño weather pattern.

However, the powerful 2026 El Niño is expected to have a significant impact in the coming year.

The research team included the University of Exeter, the University of East Anglia (UEA), CICERO Center for International Climate Research, Ludwig-Maximilian-University Munich (LMU), Alfred-Wegener-Institut and more than 90 other institutions around the world.

“A decline in fossil CO2 emissions is encouraging, but a reduction driven partly by geopolitical shocks is not a sustainable climate solution,” said Professor Pierre Friedlingstein, director of the Global Carbon Budget Office at the University of Exeter, who led the study.

“The long-term answer remains the rapid deployment of clean energy technologies. While renewables and electric vehicles are increasingly offsetting growth in energy demand, further acceleration is needed if global emissions are to peak and enter a sustained decline.”

Professor Corinne Le Quéré, Royal Society Research Professor at UEA’s School of Environmental Sciences – who led the new analysis published in Nature Climate Change – said: “Clean technologies are making a real difference in reducing global emissions. Solar and wind power and electric vehicles are expanding rapidly, led by China. Countries on every continent are decreasing their emissions while growing their economies. A further acceleration could turn the tide on global emissions within a few years.”

Glen Peters, Research Professor at the CICERO Center for International Climate Research, said: “A drop in emissions in one year is not a peak unless it is driven by structural change. Accelerated deployment of solar, wind, batteries, and electric vehicles are pushing emissions down in dozens of countries, and slowing emissions growth in many more, but the changes are not sufficient to lock in a peak in global emissions”.

Professor Julia Pongratz, at LMU’s Department of Geography, said: “Alongside reducing emissions, we need to scale up CO2 removal to reach greenhouse gas neutrality. Afforestation and reforestation already remove substantial amounts of CO2, but these gains are still more than offset by emissions from deforestation, and novel carbon removal remains very limited. We need to act on all these fronts now.”

Other key findings from the 2026 Global Carbon Budget include:

  • China’s emissions in 2026 are projected to decrease by 0.9%, following two
    years of low emissions growth. In addition to a strong decrease in oil emissions,
    coal emissions rose slightly (0.3%) and electricity generation from renewables
    continues to grow strongly.
  • While China produces 32% of global fossil emissions, it is also the country
    most active in deploying clean technologies and increasing carbon sinks
    through reforestation. Half of the global emissions avoided due to renewable
    energy and electric vehicles are in China, which also has the largest carbon
    uptake through reforestation and afforestation (new forests).
  • The USA’s emissions are projected to decrease by 1.3%, mainly due to a
    decrease in coal emissions (which spiked last year due to high natural gas prices
    and cold weather).
  • India’s emissions are projected to increase by 4.4% – higher than the 3.5%
    average annual growth over the last decade.
  • European Union emissions are projected to decrease by 1.5%.
  • Japan’s emissions are projected to decrease by 1%.
  • Emissions for the rest of the world are projected to decrease by 0.7%. The
    largest component of the decline is in oil, likely driven by increased prices and
    global supply constraints.
  • Projected 2026 emissions for the different fossil fuels are: oil down 1.9%,
    natural gas down 0.3%, coal up 0.6%.
  • Emissions from international aviation and international shipping are both
    projected to decrease by 1%. Aviation’s decline is partly due to reduced activity
    in the Middle East.
  • Global CO2 emissions from land-use change have decreased in the past decade
    but are projected to rise this year to 5.1 billion tonnes. This is driven by an
    increase in emissions from peat fires (almost entirely ignited by human
    activities) and forest degradation in Southeast Asia, associated with drier
    conditions linked to the onset of El Niño conditions.
  • Total CO2 emissions – the sum of fossil and land-use change emissions – have
    grown more slowly in the past decade (0.3% per year), compared to the previous
    decade (1.7% per year).
  • Emissions from permanent deforestation remain high at around 3.8 billion
    tonnes of CO2 per year over 2016-25. Permanent removals through
    reforestation and afforestation offset around half of the permanent
    deforestation emissions.
  • The largest carbon uptake through re/afforestation occurs in China, the EU27
    and the USA    , with combined removals 0.8 billion tonnes CO2 per year.
    Substantial uptake through re/afforestation also occurs in other regions such as
    Brazil, Russia and Indonesia – but here it is outweighed by emissions from
    deforestation.
  • Current levels of novel Carbon Dioxide Removal (excluding conventional
    means such as reforestation) currently offset less than 30 minutes of annual
    fossil CO2 emissions.
  • The concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere is set to reach 427.7 ppm in 2026,
    54% above pre-industrial levels.

The analysis published in Nature Climate Change found:

  • With global energy demand growing, new emissions can be prevented (or
    “offset”) by simultaneous growth in clean energy. The paper makes a projection
    from now to the end of 2028, finding that 64-79% of emissions from new demand
    will be offset by new solar and wind power and electric vehicles.
  • The number of countries reducing their emissions while growing their economies
    is clearly increasing: 59 countries emitted less in 2025 than before the COVID-19
    pandemic in 2019, while their economies grew.
  • Even in countries where total emissions increased over the 2019-25 period,
    growth in emissions has slowed in all regions including in China, India and other
    emerging economies.
  • The effect of electric vehicles is growing rapidly but remains small because the
    vehicle stock takes time to be replaced, with only about 10% of the car fleet
    electrified in 2025 (or 6% excluding hybrids).
  • Emissions avoided by solar and wind power rose from 0.28 billion tonnes in
    2010 to 4.1 billion tonnes in 2025, and a projected 4.6 billion tonnes in 2026.
  • The authors conclude that: “If further acceleration of clean technology
    deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in
    global CO2 emissions could be a few years away.”

The Global Carbon Budget report, produced by an international team of more than 140
scientists, provides an annual, peer-reviewed update, building on established
methodologies in a fully transparent manner.

The 2026 edition (the 21st annual report) is published in the journal Earth System
Science Data on November 10 as a pre-print, and later as a peer-reviewed paper.

The Nature Climate Change paper is entitled: “A further acceleration of clean energy is
needed for global CO2 emissions to peak and decline.”
