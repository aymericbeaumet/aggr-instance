November 23, 2026 at 3:04 PM

My first two Mac apps, [Front and Center](http://hypercritical.co/front-and-center/) and [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/), were both created back in 2020 to restore features from [DragThing](https://dragthing.com/), the venerable Mac app that was retired in 2019. The one tiny feature that [Front and Center](http://hypercritical.co/front-and-center/) provided was literally a single checkbox within DragThing’s preferences. [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/) went a bit farther, recreating one specific kind of floating palette from DragThing ([and classic Mac OS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_OS_8#Mac_OS_8.5:~:text=The%20application%20palette%20made%20its%20debut%20with%208.5)).

After [24 years](https://dragthing.com) of development, DragThing had a ton of features. People often joked that I was destined to slowly reimplement all of DragThing’s functionality in a series of small apps. Put another way, they were saying I was doomed to make strange little Mac apps with extremely limited appeal (and sales).

Perhaps to prove them wrong, the next app I created was [Hyperspace](http://hypercritical.co/hyperspace/), which provided a way to reclaim disk space *[without removing any files](http://hypercritical.co/hyperspace/#without-removing)*. Hyperspace was still a nerdy app, but at least it had *somewhat* broader appeal than my earlier efforts. Who doesn’t want to save disk space? But more importantly, it was totally unrelated to DragThing. I had broken the curse!

Alas, there was one more (small) thing from DragThing that I missed. I used to have a DragThing palette in the corner of my screen containing some commonly used folders. In earlier versions of macOS, it was possible to pin the Dock to a screen corner, but that wasn’t really what I wanted either. I just wanted a small, separate palette of folders in the corner.

This is not a hard problem to solve. Plenty of Mac apps can provide something close to what I want…but not *exactly* what I want, [in the specific way that I want it](http://hypercritical.co/2013/02/24/movies/high-maintenance.m4v). For many years, I got by with some folders in the corner of my desktop. But the problem nagged at me.

A few years ago, I went so far as to implement my desired feature in [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/), adding the ability to create a small, extra palette full of folders in addition to the app switcher. But it felt incongruous with the rest of the app, so I never shipped it. And I didn’t want to maintain my own private fork of SwitchGlass, so I abandoned that branch of the code.

## How It Started

Today, AI coding agents are helping to produce a [bounty](https://beard.fm/app/) of [new](https://www.theincomparable.com/doubleender/), [niche](https://lexontech.org/my-own-app-for-centering-notification-center-banners-on-the-mac) Mac apps, many made by people who have little or no coding experience. Even for experienced software developers, [vibe coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibe_coding) has made it possible to create apps that were previously “not worth building” due to an unfavorable ratio of effort to payoff.

It’s in this context that I revisited my “little palette of folders” idea. Surely I could vibe-code a simple app just for me in a day or two, right?

That was three and a half months ago.

## How It’s Going

So what happened? The well-known [programmer’s credo](https://www.reddit.com/r/ProgrammerHumor/comments/etboii/the_programmers_credo/) comes to mind: “We do these things not because they are easy, but because we thought they would be easy.” Indeed.

I began the project by feeding a coding agent the source code to my app, [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/), as a starting point. I wrote SwitchGlass six years ago using a substantially more primitive version of SwiftUI. (For the developers out there, it predates [@Observable](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/observation) and [@State](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/state), and it supports Macs all the way back to macOS 12 Monterey from 2021.) I asked a coding agent to modernize the code and generalize it to support an arbitrary number of palettes instead of just one app switcher palette per display.

It worked, and quickly! But it was pretty shoddy. Many optimizations and niceties from SwitchGlass had been lost. So I dove in to fix them…and maybe add one or two more features, I guess…

Now, after months of work, I’ve created a bit of a monster. But in the process, I’ve fulfilled a long-held dream of mine—a dream I had set aside years ago because I thought it was impossible. *I’ve replaced the Dock.*

## Only Apple…

If you’ve read any of [my Mac OS X reviews](http://hypercritical.co/2015/04/15/os-x-reviewed), you know that I’ve always been dissatisfied with the Dock. Over the past couple of decades, I’ve gotten used to it, but I still bump up against its limitations and undesirable behaviors every day. (Undesirable to me, anyway.)

To pick just one example, I’ve *always* wanted clicking on a folder in the Dock to open that folder in the Finder. The Dock did work this way for several years, but in 2009’s [Mac OS X 10.6 Snow Leopard](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2009/08/mac-os-x-10-6/) (…I think?), Apple switched to requiring a Command-Option-click to open a folder in the Finder. Since then, I’ve been Command-Option-clicking folders in the Dock many, many times per day. It’s a small thing, but it adds up: seventeen years of Command-Option clicking!

But what could I do? I was forced to continue to use the Dock because there are so many things that “only the Dock can do” in macOS. I was reminded of this harsh reality when I created [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/). Why doesn’t SwitchGlass show notification badges? Why does the Calendar icon show today’s date in the Dock but not in SwitchGlass? Why don’t app icons in SwitchGlass bounce when the app needs attention? Why can’t I quit an app from SwitchGlass?

Though [I did find a workaround for that last one](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/#quit), the answer to most of these questions is that third-party apps simply can’t do all the things the Dock can do. The Dock is part of the operating system, and it enjoys special privileges and deep integration that no third-party app can match.

This remains true today, especially for [sandboxed](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/app-sandbox) apps sold through the Mac App Store like SwitchGlass (and all my other Mac apps). But what about apps that *aren’t* in the Mac App Store? What about *unsandboxed* apps? How close can they come to doing all the things the Dock does, especially if they use [private APIs](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#private-apis) that are forbidden in the Mac App Store?

I aimed to find out. I hatched a plan, which I announced on [episode 706 of Accidental Tech Podcast](https://atp.fm/706) on August 26, 2026. I would create *two* apps: one [called Jetty](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#why-jetty) that would be sold through the Mac App Store, and a more powerful, unsandboxed app called Jetty *Pro* that would be distributed directly to customers, outside the Mac App Store.

But as I worked on the two apps, it became clear that “plain Jetty” was not that great. It had all the same limitations as SwitchGlass when it came to duplicating the features of the Dock. After less than two weeks of work, I abandoned plain Jetty and [concentrated entirely on Jetty *Pro*](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#why-pro).

After three and a half months of work, there are still things that only the Dock can do, but [the list is not very long](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#dock-can). I don’t expect most users to fully replace the Dock with Jetty Pro, but I’m happily doing so. If only [my 2001 self](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2001/04/macos-x/) could see me now!

## Jetty Pro

Now, finally, I’d like to introduce Jetty Pro: an app that creates customizable Dock-like floating palettes around the edges of your Mac’s displays. These palettes can contain any combination of files, folders, volumes, apps, Shortcuts, spacers, dividers, and even the Trash. If you’ve ever been unsatisfied with some aspect of how the the Dock works, or if you’ve just wanted to augment the Dock with some additional palettes, try Jetty Pro and make your Mac’s interface work the way *you* want it to. Here are some of the highlights:

- Jetty Pro palettes are extensively customizable. Nearly every palette layout metric can be adjusted. Jetty Pro can [simulate the Dock backgrounds](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#material) from macOS 15, 26, and 27, and it provides several of its own background appearances as well. The [image at the top of this post](http://hypercritical.co/2026/11/23/jetty-pro#jetty-pro-palettes) shows just some of the possibilities.

- Jetty Pro lets you [customize the icon](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#customize-item) of any item in a palette—even [the Finder](https://lmnt.me/blog/icons/finder.html). And if you’ve ever wanted to [break an app out of Squircle Jail](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#tip-squircle-jailbreak), now you can do it.

- Unlike the macOS Dock, Jetty Pro will show folder label colors (without also setting a folder symbol). You can even apply color labels to folders independent of their labels in the Finder.

- Don’t want to see “Running in Background” apps in macOS 27? Jetty Pro can [hide them](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#show-apps-running-in-background).

- Want to [hide notification badges for specific apps](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#show-notification-badges), but only in specific palettes? Jetty Pro can do that. And unlike the Dock, Jetty Pro can even hide notification badges on the System Settings app.

- Jetty Pro can [exclude or include](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#filter-apps) running apps of your choice on a per-palette basis.

- Jetty Pro lets you choose what happens when you click (or modifier-click) an [app](http://hypercritical.co/2026/11/23/jetty-pro#app-click-actions) or [folder](http://hypercritical.co/2026/11/23/jetty-pro#folder-click-actions) in a palette.

- A Jetty Pro palette can be [configured to appear on](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#appears-on) a single display, selected displays, or on every display, while also choosing which attributes, like size and position, are allowed to vary per display.

- Use Jetty Pro to augment the macOS Dock with additional palettes, or use it to [replace the Dock entirely](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#template-dock-replace). It’s up to you.

As you might have already guessed, Jetty Pro uses every [private API](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#private-apis) it can get its hands on, and it requires a ton of [special permissions](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#permissions), all of which are [explained in the documentation](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/#permissions). For sensible security reasons, macOS makes it ([increasingly](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=oqng0zjk)) cumbersome for users to grant these permissions. But Jetty Pro needs them to do what it does. Check Settings > General in Jetty Pro and make sure it says “All permissions have been granted” at the bottom. If not, click the “Details…” button for further instructions.

After a brief dalliance with more “mainstream” apps like [Hyperspace](http://hypercritical.co/hyperspace/), I’m back to making niche apps for Mac weirdos like me. To thine own self be true, I guess. I hope there are others out there who have longed to make the Dock work just *slightly* differently and are now able to do so thanks to Jetty Pro.

I’m sure many people reading this will think it’s ridiculous that I spent months battling the limitations of macOS to create what appears (to the undiscerning eye) to be a nearly identical clone of the Dock. What a waste of time! And what took so long anyway? This is a weekend project, at most!

First, I can assure you that the “simple” appearance of Jetty Pro belies the huge level of complexity and trickery required to do so many different things that macOS does not support and actively works against. If Jetty Pro really does seem simple in use, then I have done my job well. (And if you can see the seams, then that’s where I’ve failed to completely overcome a hostile system that doesn’t want to allow Jetty Pro to do what it needs to do.)

Second, I put a tremendous amount of time and effort into Jetty Pro. The [documentation](http://hypercritical.co/jetty-pro/) alone (which, like every word in every post on this website, was written by me and me alone) took weeks to create. During the lead up to Jetty Pro 1.0, I’ve released over 160 builds to my 250 beta testers—more than one build per day. The Jetty Pro app icon was created by the fine humans at [The Iconfactory](https://iconfactory.com/). From the start, I’ve treated Jetty Pro like a “real” app, not a throwaway vibe-coded project.

Having spent so many years with the Dock as a tiny pebble in my shoe, the difference between it and Jetty Pro means everything to me. Like [Front and Center](http://hypercritical.co/front-and-center/) and [SwitchGlass](http://hypercritical.co/switchglass/) before it, I’m now running Jetty Pro all day, every day on my Mac. Replacing the Dock with Jetty Pro has made me happier than any other app I’ve created. I hope it will make some other Mac weirdos happy too.
