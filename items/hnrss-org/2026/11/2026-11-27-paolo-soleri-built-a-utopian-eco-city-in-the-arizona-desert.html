In 1970 Paolo Soleri constructed Arcosanti, an experimental city in the Arizona desert that counters suburban sprawl

In 1970, Paolo Soleri began building a future-proofed eco-city in the baking Arizona desert. The Italian architect, a student of Frank Lloyd Wright, had planned for 5,000 people to inhabit Arcosanti, but in the end the population peaked at 150. Few, it seems, could face the harsh, monastic life and radical experiment in communal living

10 February 2026

From the tops of cypress trees, an assemblage of Brutalist buildings rises amid the high Sonoran Desert in Arizona – Arcosanti, Paolo Soleri’s unfinished, 860-acre prototype city, which began construction in 1970 and is more relevant today than ever. Planned for 5,000 residents, the architect’s dense urban vision favours compactness over sprawl and three-dimensionality over the flatness of suburbia, emerging from the landscape as a series of earth-cast concrete apses and vaults. In reality, numbers have never reached more than 150 and, today, just two dozen people carry on Soleri’s work. It functions more as an experiment in communal living than as the small-scale city he envisaged, but this is precisely what many ‘Arconauts’ are drawn to. They live simply, creatively and collectively. Weekly gatherings, a community room, a library, gym and farmers’ market structure their days. Manny Garcia has lived at Arcosanti for a year. ‘I feel like this is the future,’ he says. ‘Housing is so unaffordable. Co-living cuts costs and offers a built-in community.’

Soleri moved to the USA from Turin in 1946, studying under Frank Lloyd Wright at Taliesin West. The postwar economic boom saw American cities begin to grow exponentially. This impulse for expansion was mirrored by Lloyd Wright’s unrealised Broadacre City, which proposed increased development outside of urban centres so that each person might have their own acre of land. Arcosanti emerged in direct opposition, built on Soleri’s philosophy of ‘arcology’ – architecture fused with ecology – introduced in his 1969 book of that name, which is subtitled The City in the Image of Man. In it he wrote: ‘The liveliness of our world is hindered by the physical extension of our shelters.’ Jeff Stein, an architect and a director of the Cosanti Foundation, which promotes Soleri’s ideals, paraphrases thus: ‘In essence, spreading out kills culture.’

Soleri’s concerns resonate today as much as they did in 1970. Cities continue to swell, yet we are acutely aware of the damaging impact of urban expansion. With average house prices in the USA surpassing $410,000, record high building costs, unaffordable rentals and post-pandemic isolation, the recent surge in shared living arrangements begins to make sense. People are staying in the parental home or flat-sharing for longer out of necessity. Some are exploring communes and ecologically minded ‘intentional communities’. High-end manifestations of the trend, such as Ark in London’s Canary Wharf – a 21-storey residential tower with shared kitchens, work spaces, a pool, spa and rooftop restaurant – are a far cry from Soleri’s pursuit of ‘elegant frugality’, but they do signal a desire for connection.

Yvonne Brook, an artist, shares a two-bedroom unit in Arcosanti’s East Crescent Wing – a common arrangement in the development, where housing is generally assigned by a committee according to seniority, and one that offers Yvonne many advantages. ‘It’s easier to talk over cooking dinner and eating together. Having people to bounce ideas off of helps keep me moving forward,’ she says.

In October 2026, amid Britain’s housing crisis, the William Morris Gallery in London will present An Earthly Paradise, exploring 200 years of living experiments, housing activism and emerging social structures. Hadrian Garrard, the museum’s director, suggests that a broader shift away from traditional family units has contributed to the rise in alternative living arrangements. ‘Here in the West, we have moved to a more fluid way of thinking about gender identity,’ he says. Polyamory and other relationship structures are now in the mainstream… To think about how we are to live is to think about where we want to live and with whom.

‘This is by no means new but is perhaps fuelled by two contemporary factors – the ongoing global housing crises, where sharing housing costs with others just makes more sense, and an epidemic of loneliness in Western culture.’ Soleri’s question endures: how can we plan for this? In an age of digital connection but physical separation, Arcosanti remains an antidote – an experiment in density and shared humanity.

‘We survive on giving people a chance to experience that,’ says David Tollas, who arrived in 1981. He met his wife, Nadia Begin, during a workshop, and together they raised two sons among the compound’s courtyards and half-domed rooms. At first, he lived in one of the small ‘cubes’, spartan concrete dwellings scattered below the mesa. Today those early quarters resemble a ghost village. He and Nadia now occupy an earthen-domed home behind the foundry, where art, books and rolls of architectural plans fill the shelves. Much of Arcosanti’s furniture, its pictures and ceramics, are generational and have moved through various spaces. ‘The stuff in our place that has been saved over the years represents all the people who have made up the community,’ says Tollas.

Larger homes are generally assigned to longer-term residents, but sometimes people get lucky. Garcia lives alone in a five-bedroom unit and takes a ‘whatever you can get approach’ to decorating. Phoenix is an hour’s drive away, so Arconauts often rely on repurposed items left in the community space – a repository of clothes, appliances and furniture.

‘Arcosanti is traditionally about minimal living – a deliberate poverty of living simply for the sake of equity,’ says Ray Paskus, a resident of two years and an archivist for the Cosanti Foundation. Perhaps this is why so many co-living projects fail to last the journey. Arcosanti is the exception, not the rule – and it’s hardly thriving. Less than 10 per cent of Soleri’s masterplan has been realised and, despite a broader interest in communal living, Paskus says applications are at a standstill. ‘People project all sorts of things on to Arcosanti, but the lifestyle is harsh and isolating and full of hard work – it’s almost monastic. To grow the community you have to be able to sell that to people.’ There are few, he’s found, that are truly interested in the asceticism needed to make earth-conscious co-operative living work.

It’s a shame, he says, because a philosophy of reuse and collaborative living could be one solution to the ills of our time. Soleri died in 2013 at the age of 93, still convinced that ‘we must reinvent the city to develop our civilisation’. Arcosanti endures as that living sketch – unfinished, alive and quietly radical.
