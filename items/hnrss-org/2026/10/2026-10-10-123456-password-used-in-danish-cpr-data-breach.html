• Annika wins one of Denmark’s biggest music awards

    Oct 17, 2026

    ·

    The 21-year-old singer has gone from posting piano videos on TikTok to becoming one of Denmark’s biggest pop stars. On Friday, she received the top prize at DR’s annual P3 Guld music awards.

    Danish singer Annika has won P3 Prisen, the main award at one of Denmark’s most prominent annual music events, P3 Guld.

    The 21-year-old received the award Friday evening at DR Koncerthuset in Copenhagen, a year after being nominated for the same prize but losing out to Danish band Aphaca.

    P3 Guld is the annual music awards show organised by P3, the youth-oriented music and entertainment radio station run by Danish public broadcaster DR.

    The event celebrates artists who have made a significant impact on Danish music over the past year, with P3 Prisen regarded as its main award.

    Annika, whose full name is Annika Wedderkopp, was selected by a P3 jury ahead of fellow nominees Zar Paulo and producer Anton Westerlin, who is also her boyfriend.

    “With an authentic foundation in her own life, Annika creates massive hits about breakups and infatuations, about doubt and a steely belief that everything will naturally work out,” the jury said.

    From TikTok to the Danish charts

    Annika’s rise has been rapid.

    She began by posting videos of herself singing at the piano on TikTok before breaking through with “Knuser hjerter” in 2024.

    She followed it with several successful releases, including “Så længe jeg er sexy”, and won P3 Talentet, the event’s talent award, that same year.

    Last year, she released her debut album “AW”, which includes “Stolt”, a collaboration with Danish rapper Lamin. She also opened Roskilde Festival’s Orange Stage, the festival’s largest and most prestigious stage.

    Previous recipients of P3 Prisen include some of Denmark’s best-known contemporary artists, including The Minds of 99, Tobias Rahim, Artigeardit, Lamin, Tina Dickow and Jada.

    Other winners

    Several other awards were presented at Friday’s ceremony.

    Guldimund and Saveus won P3 Lytterhittet – the listeners’ choice award – for their song “Vil du noget?”.

    Zar Paulo received P3 Live, an award recognising an artist who has distinguished themselves on Denmark’s live music scene. The award returned this year after previously being presented in 2001 and 2009.

    Danish-Faroese artist Friða Saiya won P3’s Næste, a new award that has replaced P3 Talentet and highlights emerging artists.

    Criticism over lack of women

    This year’s P3 Guld also faced criticism before the ceremony over the gender balance among its performers.

    Twelve men and two women were scheduled to perform during the evening.

    Ditte Christiane Jensen, head of editorial at DR Ung, defended the lineup in a written response to Ritzau.

    “Talent, creativity and artistic quality are not about gender,” she said.

    “This year’s lineup was put together based on the artists and bands that have left a significant mark on the music year and in P3’s universe.”

    The US president says he “got it for nothing” after signing a new defence agreement with Denmark and Greenland that expands the American military presence on the island.

    US President Donald Trump has described a new defence and security agreement with Denmark and Greenland as potentially “the biggest real estate deal that has ever been made”.

    Speaking at a Republican campaign rally in Dalton, Georgia, Trump portrayed the agreement as a major achievement for the United States.

    “What do you think of the deal I made on Greenland? Got it for nothing,” Trump told supporters.

    “It is probably the biggest real estate deal that has ever been made.”

    Trump was campaigning ahead of the US midterm elections on November 3.

    His comments come after months of tension over Greenland, an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark. Trump has repeatedly said that the United States wants control and ownership of Greenland, arguing that the island is crucial to American national security.

    Two new US military bases

    The United States, Denmark and Greenland signed a new security and defence agreement on Greenland at the end of September following lengthy negotiations.

    The agreement was signed at the United Nations in New York shortly after Trump addressed the UN General Assembly.

    It allows the United States to establish two additional military bases in Greenland.

    The US currently operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland, which plays an important role in missile warning, space surveillance and Arctic security.

    The new agreement also opens the possibility of deploying parts of the planned US Golden Dome missile defence system in Greenland. The system is intended to protect the United States against threats including missiles and drones.

    Despite Trump’s description of the agreement as a “real estate deal”, it does not transfer ownership of Greenland to the United States.

    Trump’s comments came as Republicans campaign to retain control of Congress in the November midterm elections. Democrats are currently polling more strongly in the race for the House of Representatives, while control of the Senate remains more uncertain.

    Republicans currently hold majorities in both chambers.

  • Denmark’s shift from fossil fuels to electricity is accelerating

    Oct 17, 2026

    ·

    Electric cars, heat pumps and electric boilers are driving a sharp increase in the share of Denmark’s energy consumption covered by electricity.

    Denmark is becoming increasingly electrified as electric cars, heat pumps and electric boilers replace fossil fuels.

    Electricity accounted for 23.4 percent of Denmark’s total energy consumption in 2025, according to figures highlighted by industry organisation Green Power Denmark.

    That was an increase of 1.5 percentage points in just one year.

    The increase is significant. Between 2016 and 2024, the share of electricity in Denmark’s total energy consumption rose by only 3.8 percentage points.

    The acceleration is being driven particularly by the growing number of electric cars and by district heating companies investing in electric boilers and heat pumps.

    Kristian Jensen, CEO of Green Power Denmark, says the development will gradually make Denmark less dependent on imported oil and natural gas – and therefore less exposed to international energy prices and geopolitical developments.

    “We are freeing ourselves from what is going on in the Middle East and American politics,” Jensen told Ritzau.

    “When Danes experience that gasoline, diesel and electricity have become more expensive than they were a year ago, it is because we are still dependent on the price set in the rest of the world.”

    Target: 60 percent by 2035

    Green Power Denmark wants electricity to account for 60 percent of Denmark’s total energy consumption by 2035.

    Reaching that level will require a much faster transition in Danish industry, according to Jensen.

    He points to heat pumps as one of the technologies that can help companies replace natural gas with electricity. Iron foundries and food producers, including dairy group Arla, have already demonstrated that large heat pumps can be used in industrial production.

    Jensen argues that other companies should follow their example.

    “It will not only reduce the companies’ CO2 emissions, it will also reduce energy expenses and thereby improve their competitiveness,” he said.

    Figures for Denmark’s electrification rate in 2026 are not yet available, but Green Power Denmark expects the shift towards electricity to continue.

  • Denmark’s oldest-ever person was a member of the Danish Nazi party

    Oct 17, 2026

    ·

    Records show that 112-year-old Kirsten Schwalbe joined Denmark’s Nazi party during the German occupation. She says she did so at her husband’s request and did not sympathise with Nazism.

    Kirsten Schwalbe, who this week became the longest-living Dane in recorded history, was a member of Denmark’s Nazi party during the German occupation.

    The 112-year-old joined the National Socialist Workers’ Party of Denmark (DNSAP) in August 1941, according to Kristeligt Dagblad.

    Her name appears in the Bovrup registry, a record of people who were members of DNSAP. Two historians have independently confirmed to the newspaper that the entry matches Schwalbe’s date of birth and address, although her surname is misspelled as “Schalbe”.

    Schwalbe has confirmed that both she and her former husband were members of the party, but says she only joined because her husband urged her to do so.

    She rejected the suggestion that she had sided with Nazi Germany.

    “Not at all. I barely knew what it was, because I have never taken an interest in politics,” she told TV Midtvest when asked whether she had sympathised with the Nazis.

    Schwalbe said she found it unfair that she should have to “pay for it” more than 70 years later, adding that “back then, wives didn’t look into such things”.

    Membership does not show level of involvement

    DNSAP was founded in 1930 and was strongly inspired by Adolf Hitler’s German Nazi Party, the NSDAP.

    But Charlie Krautwald, a historian at the University of Copenhagen, cautioned against drawing conclusions about Schwalbe’s level of involvement from the registry alone.

    The record shows that a person joined the party, he told Kristeligt Dagblad, but not why they joined, how long they remained a member or whether they paid membership fees.

    A person could therefore appear in the registry without having been active in the party.

    TV Midtvest has followed Schwalbe for several years and manages an Instagram account about her life. Two of the broadcaster’s employees have also recently published a book about her.

    Her membership of DNSAP had not previously been mentioned. TV Midtvest editor-in-chief Marc Killigren Akselsen told Kristeligt Dagblad that the broadcaster had not known about it.

    Schwalbe, who lives in Struer in western Jutland, became the longest-living Dane on record on Wednesday, when she reached the age of 112 years and 218 days.

    The Gokstad ship is Norway’s largest preserved Viking ship.

    “The boat is a very important cultural treasure from the Viking Age, and it is not an everyday occurrence that we grant permission for such a boat to be taken out of the country,” Kaare Stang of Riksantikvaren, Norway’s cultural heritage authority, said.

    The three smaller boats had been chopped into pieces, possibly as part of a burial ritual, according to archaeologists.

    The Museum of Cultural History in Norway owns the boat and applied for permission to lend it to Moesgaard Museum for a new exhibition.

    Norwegian authorities normally approve loans for no more than three years. Moesgaard Museum must therefore submit a report on the boat’s condition every three years during the loan period.

    Stang said Norwegian authorities trusted the Danish museum to protect the boat but emphasized that it must eventually return to Norway.
    No arrival date has been announced.

    Moesgaard Museum already displays a 6.5-meter Gokstad boat that was used to transport crews and exchange orders between warships.

  • Copenhagen establishes support groups for parents of children missing school

    Oct 16, 2026

    ·

    A new parents’ association has signed a four-year agreement with the City of Copenhagen to establish support groups for families whose children experience prolonged, involuntary school absence.

    Such absence is often connected to poor well-being and is considered concerning when a pupil misses at least 20 percent of school for two consecutive quarters. That corresponds to about 10 days per quarter.

    The first groups will be funded by Copenhagen and include eight to nine parents who meet approximately once a month, education news outlet Folkeskolen.dk reported.

    A family therapist will lead the meetings, with a parent from Forældrekommissionen, or the Parents’ Commission, also participating.

    Louise Gielov and Jens Langorn helped establish the association after their family appeared in the Danish public broadcaster DR documentary “Skolens tabte børn,” which showed daily life with children unable to attend school.

    The couple received more than 1,000 messages from parents in similar situations after the documentary aired.

    The association now has 2,545 members and aims to establish parent groups across Denmark. It also plans to use families’ experiences to promote earlier intervention and broader changes to prevent school absence.

    The proportion of pupils with prolonged concerning absence increased from 2.4 percent in the 2019-2020 school year to 5.1 percent in 2024-2025.

    The association has also secured 7 million DKK from foundations.

  • Dane becomes first European participant to receive combined flu and Covid vaccine

    Oct 16, 2026

    ·

    A 65-year-old Dane will become the first person in Europe to receive a new combined influenza and Covid-19 vaccine as part of a clinical trial Friday.

    The trial will examine whether one injection can protect against both diseases as effectively as, or better than, the two separate vaccines currently used, Danish public broadcaster DR reported.

    The first participant will receive the vaccine at Gentofte Hospital.

    “We are investigating whether one shot with this new vaccine is just as effective or potentially better than the two shots given today at preventing hospitalizations with influenza and Covid-19,” said Tor Biering-Sørensen, a professor and senior physician at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital who is leading the trial.

    About 165,000 Danes have agreed to participate. More than 300,000 people are expected to join over the next two years.

    Half of the participants will receive one dose of the combined vaccine. The other half will receive separate influenza and Covid-19 vaccinations.

    People age 65 and older in Denmark are automatically invited to receive free vaccinations against both diseases. The Danish Health Authority said about 1.3 million residents received invitations in September.

    People with chronic illnesses or other conditions placing them at increased risk are also eligible for the free program.

    The vaccines are intended to reduce the risk of severe illness and hospitalization during the winter respiratory infection season.

  • Archaeologists uncover more than 1,000 graves in Copenhagen park

    ·

    Archaeologists have excavated more than 1,000 graves containing human remains beneath Hans Tavsens Park in Copenhagen’s Nørrebro district.

    The park, located next to Assistens Cemetery, was previously used as a burial ground for Copenhagen’s poorest residents. Victims of the city’s 1853 cholera epidemic are among those buried there.

    “We can use it to gain insight into the poor population from this period and Copenhagen’s history,” Museum of Copenhagen archaeologist Olivia Persson told Danish public broadcaster DR.

    The excavation is taking place before Hans Tavsens Park and the neighboring Hans Tavsens Gade are redeveloped into a connected green space.

    The project will also allow the park to collect water during cloudbursts, helping prevent flooding of nearby roads and basements as Denmark experiences wetter weather.

    Construction began in autumn 2025 as part of the City of Copenhagen’s plan for managing extreme rainfall. The redevelopment is expected to be completed in spring 2028.

    Most of the remains will be transferred to the University of Copenhagen’s Anthropological Laboratory for storage and examination. Some will undergo additional analysis, while the remainder will be reburied in the park.

    Researchers will examine the health of selected individuals. They will also study how the graves were arranged to better understand religious and cultural attitudes toward death among Copenhagen’s poorer population at the time.

    Oct 16, 2026

    ·

    Denmark’s 20 crematoria could replace oil- and gas-powered ovens with electric models if authorities approve a lower minimum temperature requirement.

    The Danish Environmental Protection Agency has proposed reducing the required temperature in cremation ovens’ post-combustion chambers from 800 to 750 degrees Celsius, engineering publication Ingeniøren reported.

    The proposed change has been submitted for public consultation.
    Electric ovens are expected to reduce both environmental effects and the financial cost of cremations.

    Michael Smidemann, chair of the Danish Crematoria Association, said crematoria were interested in replacing their existing ovens.

    “The background is a desire to sharpen the green profile, even though it is somewhat mixed how green the electricity we receive actually is,” he told Ingeniøren.

    Electric cremation ovens can reach 750 degrees. Requiring them to reach 800 degrees would demand additional heating elements, increasing electricity use and causing heavy wear on the ovens’ brickwork, according to Ingeniøren.

    Denmark recorded 58,185 deaths in 2025, according to the Danish Crematoria Association.

    Of those who died, 51,252 were cremated, corresponding to 88.1 percent of all deaths.

    A cremation typically takes about 90 minutes, according to information from the City of Copenhagen and other sources.

  • Tiny parasitic wasp discovered in Denmark for the first time

    Oct 15, 2026

    ·

    A nature enthusiast searching beneath ferns in a North Zealand forest has identified an insect species never previously recorded in the country.

    The discovery was made in mid-September at Gribskov National Nature Park during a bioblitz, a 24-hour event in which participants search for and document wildlife.

    Around 70 people took part in the event, organized in collaboration with the Vores Vilde Natur project and biodiversity platform Arter.dk.

    Nature educator Lars Holm Hansen discovered the wasp, measuring just a few millimeters, while shaking bracken ferns to examine the insects hiding underneath.

    He collected several specimens using an insect aspirator but could not immediately identify them.

    After posting a photograph on Arter.dk, he received help from experts who identified the species as Hemiptarsenus ornatus.

    “It shows that if you put in a little effort out in the field, you can get lucky, and it is simply worth the effort,” Hansen said.

    The Natural History Museum of Denmark confirmed the finding in a press release.

    Museum insect curator Lars Vilhelmsen explained that the species has previously been recorded in Germany, Sweden and Norway.

    “It has probably been here all along. It is already known from several countries around us, but it is so small that you do not just easily spot it,” Vilhelmsen said.

    He added that relatively few people in Denmark study parasitic wasps, meaning many species may remain undocumented.
