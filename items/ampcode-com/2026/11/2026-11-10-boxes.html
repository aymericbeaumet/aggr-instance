Today, we're introducing something new to Amp: boxes.

A box is an always-on computer to which you can deploy the many delightful things you've built with Amp.

Boxes are small and cheap (the smallest one is $18 per 30 days). But they're full-on computers. You can run anything you want on them. They come with an agent running on them, too. And agents are great at running on computers.

Today, with hyper-individual software being created nearly as fast as someone can dictate a prompt, a small machine goes a long way and can serve most personal or internal software. Software that you build for yourself and people you work with doesn't need to scale to thousands of users. All it needs to be is available for everyone you want to share with and within reach of an agent.

That's what boxes are: a home for what you build with your agents.

How It Works

You develop your software in orbs. You test and share work in progress in portals. When you're ready, you ask the agent to spawn a box for you.

A box is a single VM to which you can then deploy multiple applications. Each application gets its own URL. The box is permanent and will stick around, it doesn't turn off, and you can deploy to it multiple times from however many orbs you want. (If you want to save money, you can ask the agent to turn the box on and off on a schedule.)

It's worth pointing out that there are no containers or other abstractions involved. A box truly is... just a computer. Because, again, agents are great at using those.

If you want, you can make deployments private or require visitors to Sign In With Amp. Ask the agent to do it for you, it'll know how.

Shared Boxes

You can share boxes with your workspace, so you can run your internal software on a box and everyone on the team can deploy new versions.

Runners on Boxes

Each box comes with its own runner. That means you can leverage how good agents are at using computers by spawning them right there, on the box.

Ask them to clean up the team wiki's database, for example. Or add a new entry based on a screenshot you took. Or ask it to analyze the data everyone added.

Why build elaborate admin interfaces when you can just ask the agent, right there, to modify the state of the running applications?
