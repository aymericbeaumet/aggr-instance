I've been called a Thought Leader.

I don't love the term. But I've used it, because there wasn't another one. When PMI-Agile Alliance introduces me, when conference bios get written, when someone asks what kind of writing I do. Thought leadership is the label that fits the slot. It's not wrong. It's just not quite right.

Three times in my career — at Cutter Consortium, at Thoughtworks, and from Martin Fowler reviewing a manuscript — someone in a position to know told me the same thing: let your voice out. That it kept needing to be said tells you something about the default pressure.

Martin is a co-author of the Agile Manifesto, one of the most respected voices in software development, and someone who has been writing about technical and organizational topics on martinfowler.com for twenty years. His feedback on my Wild West to Agile book was direct: put more of yourself in. I was holding back. I'd written a software history book when I had a memoir trying to get out. Martin saw the gap before I did.

What he was asking for had a character, a way of writing that neither of us named at the time. I've been thinking about what to call it ever since.

Three ways to disappear

Thought leadership started as a real thing. It meant ideas worth following. Insight that moved a field.

Then it became a content category. Then a marketing function. Then a LinkedIn post with a hook, three lessons, and a call to follow for more. The corruption was gradual and then total. Now “thought leadership content” is a phrase that means almost nothing, or worse, signals the opposite of what it claims. Polish over substance. Brand over argument. Engagement over truth.

The academic route looked like the alternative. If you wanted to be taken seriously, you built the scaffolding. Citations. Caveats. Literature reviews. You buried the lead because that's what rigor looked like. You suppressed your own voice because the author wasn't supposed to show. The problem isn't academic rigor. The problem is when rigor becomes camouflage.

I've done versions of both. I write for PMI where I sit on a thought leadership advisory group. I've written six books. But from the beginning I kept looking for ways to stay in the frame — mountaineering as an analogy for software development in Adaptive Software Development, dialogue to open each chapter of Agile Project Management, interviews with the founders of the major methodologies in Agile Software Development Ecosystems because I wanted their voices, not merely a summary of their ideas. The pressure to conform was real. I was lucky to work with people who either encouraged my instincts or didn't push back on them. That's rarer than it should be.

The problem isn't academic writing or thought leadership individually. At their worst, both do the same thing: they edit consequential human judgment out of the prose. The practitioner disappears behind the rigor or the polish, and what remains is correct but weightless. Academic writing hides behind rigor. Thought leadership hides behind polish. Now a third option has arrived — LLM Voice — that doesn't require hiding at all, because there's no one there. That was a problem worth solving before AI arrived. Now it's urgent.

To come out of hiding, I propose a new writing category: Practitioner Voice.

What Practitioner Voice does

Academic writing builds to the claim. Thought leadership makes the claim and explains it. Practitioner Voice starts with the claim and trusts the reader to close the gap. The author's judgment stays visible throughout. The whole piece organized around what the author has concluded from experience, not what the evidence might tentatively suggest.

Underneath all of it sits judgment. You can learn the moves. The judgment has to be earned.

Martin Fowler writes this way. His best pieces don't build to the conclusion, they open with it. The rest is a practitioner showing his reasoning, not constructing a case for a jury. The reader either recognizes the pattern from their own experience or doesn't. He doesn't write for the ones who don't.

When Martin pushed me to put more of myself into Wild West to Agile, he was pushing me toward this writing category. He recognized it before I had a name for it — and he knew the book needed it because the alternative was a history that anyone could have written. Practitioner Voice is the thing only you can write, because it's built from what only you have lived.

What makes it different

Four things separate Practitioner Voice from the alternatives.

Authority from experience, not credentials. Academic authority comes from credentials and methods. Thought leadership authority comes from platform and reputation. Practitioner authority comes from having been in the situation, not as a researcher studying it, but as a person responsible for the outcome. That difference shows in every sentence. You can't fake it and you can't credential your way into it. For consultants the authority takes a slightly different form. We rarely carry direct accountability for outcomes. But if we do our work well, we've been close enough to consequence, across enough contexts and enough years, that the pattern recognition is real. Earned differently. Still earned.

Tension without resolution. Academic writing resolves tension — that's what conclusions are for. Thought leadership resolves it in the three takeaways. Practitioner Voice names the tension and leaves it live. The practitioner knows the tension is real and the reader has to navigate it themselves. Resolving it for them would be lying.

The author stays in. Academic writing never allows this. Thought leadership allows it only as personal brand. In Practitioner Voice the author's judgment is the point. Not the data. Not the framework. The accumulated perspective of someone making consequential decisions for a long time, with something to say about what that's actually like.

The readership contract. Academics write for peer reviewers. Thought leadership writes for eyeballs. Practitioner Voice writes for people who recognize the pattern from their own experience. It's comfortable with limited context — this worked here, under these conditions, judge for yourself whether it fits yours. It doesn't chase universality. It trusts specificity.

Why it matters now

This isn't new behavior. Scroll LinkedIn or Substack and you'll find it: practitioners writing from earned judgment, starting with the claim, keeping the tension live. It's been building for a decade. Nobody called it anything. Without the category, there was no way to teach it, no way to recognize it, no way to say: that's the thing, do more of that.

Now AI produces competent writing at scale — fluent, well-organized, correctly structured, optimized to say nothing wrong. Call it what it is: LLM Voice. The most insidious failure mode of the three because it's indistinguishable from the real thing until you notice nothing is at stake.

AI can mimic the sound of Practitioner Voice. It cannot own the consequences behind it. That's the distinction that holds: not style, not compression, not even the rhythm of earned judgment. Accountability. The willingness to put your name on a call and live with what happens next. That can't be automated.

Practitioner Voice survives the current moment not because it's protected from AI, but because the thing it's made of — accumulated consequence-bearing experience stated plainly — has to be earned. There's no shortcut and no simulation that closes the gap.

I've been doing this in my PMI articles for the last few years. Not loudly. Not by announcing a new approach. Just by not editing myself out, leaving the judgment visible, letting the tension stand, trusting the reader. If something in those pieces felt different to you, that was deliberate. Consider this the explanation.

We've been doing this for years. We just called it something else because we didn't have a better name.

Naming the category is the first step. The craft — a guide to writing in Practitioner Voice — is my next contribution, which I will add to this article when it's ready.
