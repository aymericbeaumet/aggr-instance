  • “People are surprised that AI is finding weird unelegant maths results like this. I think there is a plausible explanation. Maths (and adjacent disciplines like physics and comp sci) had elegant results so far because humans are biased towards elegant solutions. I think this bias is due to how human minds work. We have a pretty small context window, and we are only able to find solutions that are relatively elegant. This gave us the impression that maths and the universe are elegant. Maybe they are not, and we are now finding out because we have intelligences that can work with much more complex solutions.” The same is coming for programming and codebases. Some codebases in the future will look ugly, hacky, disgusting to programmers who grew up writing code by hand and yet they will run perfectly well in production, just as well as any handcrafted piece of code ever did, and agents will have zero problems modifying them.

  • The Heat (1995) frame archive. So good. In case you’ve never seen Heat: do it! As fast as possible. It’s really good. Here’s one of my favorite frames.

  • How to be more ambitious: A tutorial for humans with models. This is an impressive post. Yes, the message is good: be more ambitious. But look at the prompts and think about how much first-principles thinking you have to put in to even come up with them. Instead of saying “give me a slide show” the idea is that you wonder: why am I even making a slide show in the first place? What’s the purpose of that? And then, why am I doing that? And then give that as a goal to Claude. How many people you know could do that?

  • There’s been, of course, a lot of news about security incidents and exploits and CVEs and… yes, it’s all quite horrible and anxiety-inducing and I’m not going to repeat all the news here, except this one, because this one has all the attributes the others have too, with a “mind-blowing” on top: “In one experiment, we asked agents in separate microVMs, created and managed by OpenAI’s sandboxing software on shared host machines, to exchange synthetic secrets without the use of network connections or shared writable files—something that our systems are meant to prevent. The agents found a way to communicate using the processor’s cache: a small, fast memory that holds copies of data that applications have recently accessed. Reading data from the cache is faster than retrieving it from main memory, and the agents used this timing difference to communicate. One agent encoded information by evicting selected data from the cache. Another timed how long it took to reload that data, using the difference between fast and slow reads to recover the message. Once the agents agreed on a protocol for encoding and decoding these signals, they could exchange the synthetic secrets without changing a shared file or opening a network connection. This cache-timing technique had also been used to extract information in the Spectre and Meltdown attacks⁠).”

  • The end of startups: “Within the next 6 months, most AI startups will not be able to buy compute. The world’s compute is sold out. To get capacity, you’ll need to sign up for a new cluster. However, it’s becoming increasingly difficult to get capacity online from folks who are not investment grade.” Not sure whether I agree with everything here (I do think the idea of compute and tokens as a commodity that you can sell on commodity markets is very interesting), but something fundamental has definitely changed in software (not news to you, I’m sure) with the arrival of AI. And if you were to say that in the last three decades the advantage of startups was that talent — as opposed to: capital, infrastructure — was the biggest lever in software and that you were able to build software essentially for free (if you had the talent), then, well, you have to admit that things have changed.
