Room 641A's exterior

**Room 641A** is a telecommunication interception facility operated by [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T "AT&T") for the U.S. [National Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Agency "National Security Agency"), as part of an [American mass surveillance program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_surveillance_in_the_United_States "Mass surveillance in the United States"). The facility commenced operations in 2003, and its purpose was publicly revealed by AT&T technician [Mark Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Klein "Mark Klein") in 2006.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-WiredEvidence-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-wired1-2)

Room 641A is located in the [SBC Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SBC_Communications "SBC Communications") building at 611 Folsom Street, [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco "San Francisco"), three floors of which were occupied by [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T "AT&T") before SBC purchased AT&T.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-WiredEvidence-1) The room was referred to in internal AT&T documents as the *SG3 \[Study Group 3\] Secure Room*.

The room measures about 24 by 48 feet (7.3 by 14.6   m) and contains several racks of equipment, including a [Narus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narus_Inc. "Narus Inc.") STA 6400, a device designed to intercept and analyze Internet communications at very high speeds.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-WiredEvidence-1) It is fed by [fiber optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_fiber "Optical fiber") lines from [beam splitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_splitter "Beam splitter") installed in fiber optic trunks carrying [Internet backbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_backbone "Internet backbone") traffic.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-kleinex-3) In the analysis of J. Scott Marcus, a former [CTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_technology_officer "Chief technology officer") for [GTE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GTE "GTE") and a former adviser to the [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission "Federal Communications Commission"), it has access to all Internet traffic that passes through the building, and therefore "the capability to enable surveillance and analysis of internet content on a massive scale, including both overseas and purely domestic traffic."[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-marcus1-4)

The existence of the room was revealed by former AT&T technician [Mark Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Klein "Mark Klein") and was the subject of a 2006 [class action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_action "Class action") lawsuit by the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation "Electronic Frontier Foundation") against AT&T.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-EFFdocs-5) Klein claims he was told that similar [black rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_room "Black room") are operated at other facilities around the country.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-WiredVindicate-6)

Room 641A and the controversies surrounding it were subjects of an episode of *[Frontline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontline_\(U.S._TV_series\) "Frontline (U.S. TV series)")*,[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-7) the current affairs documentary program on [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Service "Public Broadcasting Service"). It was originally broadcast on May 15, 2007. It was also featured on PBS's *[NOW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOW_on_PBS "NOW on PBS")* on March 14, 2008. The room was also covered in the PBS *[Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_\(American_TV_series\) "Nova (American TV series)")* episode "The Spy Factory".

The [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation "Electronic Frontier Foundation") (EFF) filed a class-action lawsuit, *[Hepting v. AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepting_v._AT&T "Hepting v. AT&T")*, against the company on January 31, 2006, accusing the telecommunication company of violating the law and the privacy of its customers by collaborating with the [National Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Agency "National Security Agency") (NSA) in a massive, illegal program to [wiretap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiretap "Wiretap") and [data-mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_mining "Data mining") Americans' communications. On July 20, 2006, a federal judge denied the government's and AT&T's motions to dismiss the case, chiefly on the ground of the [state secrets privilege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_secrets_privilege "State secrets privilege"), allowing the lawsuit to go forward. On August 15, 2007, the case was heard by the [Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Ninth_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit") and was dismissed on December 29, 2011, based on a [retroactive grant of immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act_of_1978_Amendments_Act_of_2008 "Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 Amendments Act of 2008") by Congress for telecommunications companies that cooperated with the government. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-eff-hepting-8)

A separate case filed by the Electronic Frontier Foundation was titled *[Jewel v. NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewel_v._NSA "Jewel v. NSA")* and was filed on September 18, 2008. After many years of litigation, on April 25, 2019, the ruling from the Northern District of California [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-JewelvNSA-9) concluded that the evidence presented by the plaintiff's experts was insufficient: "the Court confirms its earlier finding that Klein cannot establish the content, function, or purpose of the secure room at the AT&T site based on his own independent knowledge." The ruling noted that "Klein can only speculate about what data were actually processed and by whom in the secure room and how and for what purpose, as he was never involved in its operation." The Court further went on to discredit other experts called upon, citing their heavy reliance on the Klein declaration.

In the spring of 2006, over 50 other lawsuits were filed against various telecommunications companies in response to the article.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-10) There has been speculation that several rooms similar to this exist all over the United States.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-11)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_641A#cite_note-12)
