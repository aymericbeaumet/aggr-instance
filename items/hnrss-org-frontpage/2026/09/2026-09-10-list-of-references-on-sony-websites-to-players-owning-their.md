\
 Four California [PlayStation](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/PlayStation "PlayStation") buyers who each spent hundreds of dollars on digital goods over the past few years say they received only a limited, revocable license rather than ownership, and paid more than they otherwise would have.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The complaint alleges the PlayStation Store advertises digital games with ownership language such as *"Buy Now"* & *"Confirm Purchase,"* and that the brief license disclosure above the *"Confirm Purchase"* button appears in comparatively small, unhighlighted text, short of the clear and conspicuous disclosure California law requires.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) On June 18, 2026, Andrew Garcia, Edward Heycock, Jason Mendoza & John Salinas sued [Sony](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony "Sony") Interactive Entertainment, LLC in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, Case No. 3:26-cv-06016, alleging the storefront's checkout language violates California's digital-goods transparency law.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)[\[2\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-aftermath-buynow-2) On August 21, 2026, Sony moved to compel individual arbitration under the PlayStation [Terms of Service](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Terms_of_service "Terms of service") and, in the alternative, to dismiss the complaint with prejudice.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)[\[4\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-aftermath-court-4) Under a heading reading *"Reasonable Consumers Would Not Be Misled,"* Sony argued that it is not plausible that reasonable consumers believed they were obtaining ownership of a digital game.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3) The case remains active, with a hearing scheduled for October 1, 2026.[\[5\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-toms-5)[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)

The complaint alleges the PlayStation Store markets digital games to shoppers with ownership language.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) When a consumer proceeds to checkout, the store displays a confirmation screen with a button labeled *"Confirm Purchase,"* and selecting it finalizes the transaction & charges the payment method.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Above that button, PlayStation includes a brief disclosure referencing the Software Product License Agreement, stating that the consumer's purchase of the digital product amounts to a license subject to that agreement.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The complaint says the disclosure appears in comparatively small text relative to other portions of the interface & is not visually highlighted or otherwise designed to draw the consumer's attention.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The transaction grants a limited, non-exclusive, revocable license governed by Sony's Software Product License Agreement, which the complaint says *"expressly provides that the software is 'licensed to you, not sold.'"*[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) That agreement bars the buyer from renting, sublicensing, copying, reverse engineering, or transferring the game, and access can depend on the continued availability of the PlayStation platform.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)

[![](https://consumerrights.wiki/thumb.php?f=Sony-playstation-lawsuit-complaint-licensed-not-sold.png&width=1680)](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/File:Sony-playstation-lawsuit-complaint-licensed-not-sold.png)

Paragraph 56 of the complaint states the PlayStation Software Product License Agreement expressly provides that the software is *"licensed to you, not sold."*[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)

KitGuru, reporting on Sony's fiscal earnings report as collated by Push Square, said 85% of game sales in the quarter ending March 31, 2026 were digital, with 15% physical.[\[6\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-kitguru-6) On July 1, 2026, Sony announced it would [end physical disc production](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_discontinues_physical_media_for_PlayStation-brand_hardwares "Sony discontinues physical media for PlayStation-brand hardwares") for new PlayStation games starting January 2028, moving new releases to digital distribution.[\[7\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-psblog-7) Fortune noted that Sony once championed the sharing of physical media and now argues that customers do not own the digital games they purchase.[\[8\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-fortune-8)

The complaint alleges that the four named plaintiffs are California residents who have each spent hundreds of dollars on PlayStation digital goods over the past few years.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Recent purchases listed for Garcia include NBA 2K25 Standard Edition for $20.99, NBA 2K26 for $65.99 & EA Sports Madden NFL 26 for $69.99.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Those listed for Heycock include Resident Evil Requiem on February 25, 2026 for $69.99.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Those listed for Mendoza include Resident Evil Requiem on February 14, 2026 for $69.99 & God of War Sons of Sparta for $29.99, and many others.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Those listed for Salinas include Five Nights at Freddy's 4 for $7.99 & Puppet Combo Collection 1 for $17.99, and many others.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Each plaintiff states he was not aware the games were only a revocable license, and that the belief he was buying ownership led him to pay more than he otherwise would have.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed Sony Corporation of America on August 20, one day before Sony's motion, leaving Sony Interactive Entertainment, LLC as the sole defendant.[\[5\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-toms-5)

Filed by Noah Heinz & Albert Pak of Pak Heinz PLLC, the complaint brings three counts.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Count I alleges a violation of California Business and Professions Code § 17500.6, the operative section of AB 2426.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)[\[9\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-ab2426-9) Count II alleges a violation of California's False Advertising Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17500 et seq.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Count III alleges a violation of the California Consumer Legal Remedies Act, Cal. Civ. Code § 1750 et seq., citing subsections 1770(a)(5), (a)(9) & (a)(14).[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) Before suing, plaintiffs' counsel sent CLRA demand letters to Sony's addresses by certified mail on April 21, 2026 & again on May 11, 2026.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)

The proposed class covers California residents who purchased digital video games through the PlayStation Store after § 17500.6's effective date, who saw representations such as *"Buy," "Buy Now,"* or *"Confirm Purchase,"* but who received only a revocable license to a digital good.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The complaint invokes federal jurisdiction under the Class Action Fairness Act, stating the aggregate amount in controversy exceeds $5,000,000.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1) The prayer for relief seeks actual damages, restitution & disgorgement of Sony's revenues from the practice, statutory & actual damages under Cal. Civ. Code § 1780(a), an injunction prohibiting Sony from resuming its unlawful practices, and reasonable attorneys' fees & costs.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)

[![](https://consumerrights.wiki/thumb.php?f=Sony-playstation-lawsuit-complaint-buy-now.png&width=1680)](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/File:Sony-playstation-lawsuit-complaint-buy-now.png)

The complaint alleges the PlayStation Store uses ownership language such as *"Buy Now"* and *"Confirm Purchase"* to induce consumers to complete digital game transactions.[\[1\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-complaint-1)

On August 21, 2026, Sony replied, first asking the court to compel arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act and stay proceedings, and only in the alternative asking the court to dismiss the complaint with prejudice under Rules 12(b)(1) and 12(b)(6) for lack of standing and failure to state a claim.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3) Sony pointed to Section 1 of what the motion calls the Software Product Licensing Agreement, which it quoted as stating that *"The Software is licensed to you, not sold."*[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)[\[10\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-pushsquare-10) The motion says the PlayStation Terms of Service put a binding arbitration agreement and a class action waiver in Section 14, and quotes the opt-out clause:

> *YOU HAVE THE RIGHT TO OPT OUT OF BINDING ARBITRATION AND CLASS ACTION WAIVER WITHIN 30 DAYS.*[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)

The clause requires a user who does not wish to be bound to notify Sony in writing within 30 days of accepting the agreement.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)[\[5\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-toms-5) Sony told the court that no plaintiff opted out.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)

Sony's fallback merits argument appears under a heading titled *"Reasonable Consumers Would Not Be Misled."*[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3) In its filing, Sony wrote:

> *In the digital age, it is not plausible to allege that reasonable consumers believed they were obtaining "ownership" of a digital game. Were that the case, then Plaintiff Edward Heycock would not have been able to obtain the game Resident Evil Requiem on February 25, 2026 for $69.99 from the PlayStation Store after Plaintiff Jason Mendoza had obtained Resident Evil Requiem on February 14, 2026, because Mr. Mendoza, not Sony, would have owned it then.*[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)[\[4\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-aftermath-court-4)

[![](https://consumerrights.wiki/thumb.php?f=Sony-playstation-lawsuit-ownership-not-plausible.png&width=1680)](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/File:Sony-playstation-lawsuit-ownership-not-plausible.png)

The argument appears on page 12 of Sony's motion, in the same paragraph as the license clause the filing quotes as *"The Software is licensed to you, not sold."*[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)

The filing extended the point to games played against other people, arguing that because digital video games are played against other players who themselves must each have a copy of the game in order to play, it is not plausible that a reasonable consumer would believe that once they obtained a digital video game they would be its exclusive owner.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3) The motion notices a hearing for October 1, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. before Judge Vince Chhabria in Courtroom 3 at 450 Golden Gate Avenue in San Francisco.[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3)[\[5\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-toms-5)

## Add-on content requires a game Sony argues buyers do not own

\[ [edit](https://consumerrights.wiki/index.php?title=Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit&veaction=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Add-on content requires a game Sony argues buyers do not own") | [edit source](https://consumerrights.wiki/index.php?title=Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section's source code: Add-on content requires a game Sony argues buyers do not own") \]

In the lawsuit, Sony argues that buyers *"do not own the product"* they buy from the PlayStation Store, and that it is not plausible to allege that reasonable consumers believed they were obtaining *"ownership"* of a digital game.[\[11\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sony-tos-11)[\[3\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sie-motion-3) Sony's own support pages tell those same buyers how to upgrade the *"digital PS4 game you already own,"* and require that game before its add-on content will work.[\[12\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sony-upgrade-12)[\[13\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sony-support-addon-13) The key phrase in each screenshot below is boxed in red.

Sony's PS4-to-PS5 upgrade support page tells buyers how to upgrade the *"digital PS4 game you already own"* to the PS5 version.[\[12\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-sony-upgrade-12)

Sony's troubleshooting page for digital downloads states "If the game is owned by someone else..." while describing licenses [\[14\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-14)

AB 2426, authored by Assembly member Jacqui Irwin, was approved by the Governor on September 24, 2024 & added Section 17500.6 to the Business and Professions Code as Chapter 513, effective January 1, 2025.[\[2\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-aftermath-buynow-2)[\[9\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-ab2426-9)[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21) The operative clause reads:

> *It shall be unlawful for a seller of a digital good to advertise or offer for sale a digital good to a purchaser with the terms "buy," "purchase," or any other term which a reasonable person would understand to confer an unrestricted ownership interest in the digital good, or alongside an option for a time-limited rental, unless either of the following occur...*[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21)

[![](https://consumerrights.wiki/thumb.php?f=Sony-playstation-lawsuit-bpc-17500-6.png&width=1320)](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/File:Sony-playstation-lawsuit-bpc-17500-6.png)

California Business and Professions Code Section 17500.6 makes it unlawful to advertise or offer a digital good for sale with the terms *"buy"* or *"purchase,"* or any other term a reasonable person would understand to confer an unrestricted ownership interest, absent the required license disclosure.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21)

The statute gives a seller two ways to comply.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21) The seller may obtain an affirmative acknowledgment from the purchaser at the time of each transaction, indicating that the buyer is receiving a license, a complete list of the restrictions & conditions of that license, and, where applicable, that the seller may unilaterally revoke access to the digital good if it no longer holds a right to it.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21) Alternatively, the seller may provide, before the transaction, a clear-and-conspicuous statement in plain language that *"buying"* or *"purchasing"* the digital good is a license, including a hyperlink, QR code, or similar method to reach the terms and conditions giving full details on the license.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21) Either disclosure must be *"distinct and separate from any other terms and conditions of the transaction"* that the purchaser acknowledges or agrees to.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21) The law exempts subscription access, digital goods offered for no monetary consideration, and goods the seller cannot revoke after the transaction, such as those made available for permanent offline download.[\[21\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-bpc17500.6-21)

Federal regulators & lawmakers raised the ownership-versus-license question before this suit.[\[22\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-ftc-22)[\[23\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-wyden-23) On April 17, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission published a consumer alert by Amy Hebert titled *"Do you really own the digital items you paid for?"* that told readers what they get on clicking the *"buy"* button *"is often merely a license to access the content,"* explained in fine print the seller can usually change.[\[22\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-ftc-22) On February 25, 2025, Senator Ron Wyden urged the FTC to stop companies from misleadingly offering people the ability to *"buy"* digital TV, e-books, movies, music & video games when consumers do not have total control over what they paid for, and called for disclosure *"before and at the point of sale."*[\[23\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-wyden-23)

[![](https://consumerrights.wiki/thumb.php?f=Sony-playstation-lawsuit-ftc-alert.png&width=1320)](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/File:Sony-playstation-lawsuit-ftc-alert.png)

The Federal Trade Commission's April 2024 consumer alert warns that what a buyer gets on clicking *"buy"* for a digital product is *"often merely a license to access the content."*[\[22\]](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/Sony_PlayStation_digital_game_ownership_lawsuit#cite_note-ftc-22)

Additional evidence has been collected from Sony-owned websites showing Sony and PlayStation using terms such as **"own," "owner," "buy," "purchase,"** and **"yours to keep"** when referring to digital games and digital content.

This material is currently **under review for inclusion as additional evidence** regarding how Sony has represented digital purchases and ownership to consumers.
