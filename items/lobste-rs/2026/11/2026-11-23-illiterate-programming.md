My family comes from a part of the world where writing is held in very high esteem. Our poets are national heroes, our role-models are dissident essayists, and our governments, unlike those in Australia and New Zealand, had the good grace to actually attempt to persecute writers because, if nothing else, they knew that those writers would be read. Even now, it's still a cultural expectation that a middle-class Polish adult is at least a little well-read, and they should be able to identify and have read at least three or four poets.

It's probably not a surprise, then, that I grew up surrounded by books and grew into a voracious reader as a child. I've always been good at reading and read a lot and, once I'd read enough, I turned out to be a pretty damned effective writer as well. Reading and writing have, in short, always been a massive part of my life to the extent that I almost did an arts conjoint with my engineering degree and when I was dissuaded from that by my parents (one of the biggest regrets in my life, if I'm to be honest), I still did my absolute best to keep up with the humanities as I did my engineering degree (I spent altogether too much time in the library trying to unpick the Latin of the Vulgate bible). And my literacy, little though you might expect it, did me some considerable favours. Being able to write well and lucidly saved me in a couple of engineering courses where I was struggling with the maths, I suspect, and I was able to contribute to magazines and online publications which kept me socially connected despite being a socially awkward mess.

It was a jolt, therefore, when I started my third job and found myself, for the first time, interacting with actual programmers. My first job was for an acoustics consultancy, and I was more than a little lucky with it, truth be told: the place was full of sensitive and intelligent music-lovers, some of whom were literary and the rest of whom were at least interested in *something* worthwhile. My second job involved working heavily with biologists: again, quite a literate crowd, even if some of them were wilfully obtuse about the humanities in the way scientists often are. My third job, though? Nope. Never have I had the misfortune to spend so much time with wilfully ill-read, obtuse, uncultured philistines. And this isn't just me complaining that they don't read Eliot or what-have-you: they had only the barest familiarity with Shakespeare, the bible or any novel at all. Hell, they thought Brandon Sanderson unbearably intellectual, and Sanderson is pretty pulpy as an author.

*Writing is often a precarious way to make a living, both directly and indirectly. While our situation is stabilising, it's a slow process, which means that if you derive value for my writing, I fear I must ask, as always, for your support. If you'd like to contribute to helping keep me writing, then, you can do so via [Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/c/IrisMeredithandherblog), [Liberapay](https://en.liberapay.com/iris_meredith/) or [Stripe](https://donate.stripe.com/6oE2a30hh3my2reeUY).*

In short: programmers don't read, and this vexes me sorely. The immediate irritation is simply that I feel quite lonely: in being fairly literate and writing about it, I quite often find myself labelled as suspect or as "not a real tech person": that kind of problem, you know? More problematically, I genuinely find it hard to build a rapport with your average non-reading programmer. And I am not bad at communicating: if I was, quite frankly, this blog would not be a thing, nor would it be half as well-read as it is. No, there's something about the non-reading programmer that, for want of a better word, makes it difficult to communicate with them: try as I might, my best attempts mostly bounce off them entirely.

Which poses an issue. After all, I'd probably not be offended if a roofer or an electrician didn't read, at least not beyond the general kind of "bemoaning societal illiteracy" that I tend to engage in. My general assumption with tradies or people outside my general literati bubble is that they don't need to be reading lots of hard books in order to lead happy, fulfilling and socially valuable lives. I don't think that my plumber needs to read Goethe in order to be able to fix my toilet well. And yet with programmers, not reading sticks in my head as a massive moral defect. Why?

Some light can be shone on the matter by the fact that the best programmers and engineers (as opposed to the general run of the profession) buck the general trend and read quite a lot: Efron Licht, to give just one example, reads quite widely and in real depth. Even if they don't quite engage in the way that I'd like them to, they make the effort, and consequently they usually have something interesting to say. The engineers that don't read, by contrast, I've always found very difficult to talk to productively.

As it happens, this generalises. The better a software engineer is at programming (and I mean this both in a moral and in a technical sense), the wider a range of interests they tend to have. Good programmers do all matter of different weird and wonderful things, while the kind of programmer who's only interested in programming and tech in general tends to be a second-rate software developer at best. What this suggests to me is that a large part of my issue is that not reading bespeaks incuriosity. As some long-forgotten Chinese poet had it:

> The powerful don't read, The illiterate are loaded, Fuck up and you'll get promoted
>
> *Yuan Dynasty, attribution lost*

The complaint being made here is quite similar to mine: powerful and influential people lack the most basic curiosity about the world and things beyond their immediate sphere, and it shows. Importantly, the issue is that of power, as the poet doesn't complain about peasants or artisans not reading. This is an insight of some importance.

Programmers as a class have occupied, and often still do (mass layoffs aside) a position of considerable cultural power in our society. And a lot of them don't read. They don't wish to learn about times and places other than their own. They are basically dull-minded and incurious people, which makes them manifestly unfit for the position of power they occupy.

After all, being in a position of power means that you're in a position to make choices that impact other people, often quite a lot of them. If you're a software developer writing an interface, your choices will influence how other people interact with the software you've written. If you're writing accounting software, you influence whether people get access to loans or not. Your decisions have a lot of influence on how people interact with the world.

To do this task well, you need to be able to exercise curiosity about other people and how they live. You need to be able to learn what's important to them and what isn't, how they work, what they find confusing... while one might possibly (if not plausibly) learn to exercise exactly the kind of curiosity needed for your work and *only that curiosity*, it's far easier just to start by being curious in general. Lack of curiosity, as indicated by an unwillingness to read, thus indicates that you're bad at your job.

So, that's one part of the irritation: what's the rest?

## Failure to communicate

The incuriosity is, in itself, bad enough, but it doesn't explain the whole phenomenon. One could, after all, imagine programmers doing work similar to that of plumbing: writing glue code that's important for infrastructure but that doesn't really require much communication with other people (although I think you'll find that even plumbing and electrical work take quite a bit of communication: it's just that even the worst communicator is able to communicate "the toilet broke and is spewing sewerage everywhere" pretty well mosst of the time). Unfortunately, code doesn't really work that way.

Any act of programming or producing code is an act of written communication, and doing it well exercises much the same skills as writing an essay does. To give one example of this, wide structure in a codebase involves basically the same considerations as wide structure in an essay: you want to make sure that different subjects are separated out correctly and each subject is placed in a logical grouping with other subjects of its kind. Similarly, you want to make sure that these groupings are logically arranged together in a way that flows well and leaves the reader edified at the end. A codebase, in short, is a written communication to a reader first and something executed by a machine second.

This is easily enough evidenced: unmaintainable or unreadable code is, in the long run, useless, no matter how effective it might be initially. If you can't change the code and adapt it to fit your situation, or if you can't read it to see what it does, it doesn't matter what it *does* do: it's mostly useless to you. The basic functions of code, then, are quite fundamentally tied up with its role as a written communication directed at other people.

And here's the rub: to be good at communicating, curiosity is essential. After all, when you write to communicate, you have to imagine yourself, at least partially, in the reader's head: to figure out how they'd interpret a given set of words, to consider the connotation of what's written (butt-dial and booty call are, after all, denotatively identical) and to make *ethos*, *pathos* and *logos* work together for your particular audience. This applies to technical writing just as much as to the writing of fiction or essays. You have to have a healthy curiosity about who your audience are and what they're like, otherwise your writing is simply *bad*.

And this, I think, is the second great sin of the non-reading programmer: they feel entitled to do what they will without putting any effort into either communicating or being communicated with. It puts me in mind of the job interviews I've been in where I've been told that I'm excellent technically and communicate well, but that I "lack relatability": what this means, I think, is that by virtue of who I am, your average programmer will have to put a little effort into communicating with me. It's only a little, and it's the amount that implicitly exists when you're communicating with someone who isn't exactly like you, but even that mote is too much for a non-reading programmer.

The consequences are as you might expect: they range from bad documentation and unreadable code on the relatively trivial end, to the fact that people are willing to work for OpenAI on the grossly immoral end of things. Programmers like this are, quite honestly, just horrible to work with, let alone spend time with. And aside from these problems, there are half-a-hundred other little irritations that spin off from them: the weird rationalist cults, the repeated reinvention of eugenics and phrenology, DHH...

Unfortunately, our non-reading colleagues have one major advantage over us in the job market in that they can usually be relied on to smile and nod and say "yes, boss" to whatever inane diktats have come down from management on any given day. It doesn't matter that the code will be an unreadable, unmaintainable mess, nor that the request may, in point of fact, have been physically impossible: the high-ups, who by and large also don't like reading, nor communicating or being communicated with, see work happening, so they're happy. Us unlucky folks, who feel the need to communicate and be communicated with, are shit out of luck.

It's too much to hope for, alas, that this problem might be fixed by forcing the non-reading programmer to read a novel a month or whatever: high school English classes show what happens when you try that. Still, it wouldn't hurt to try a bit of social engineering: weird manosphere shit was able to get people to read (and horribly mangle) Marcus Aurelius, so getting people to read Shakespeare should be doable. Creating the expectation that engineers should be cultured seems worth a try, and it could be quite doable by people like me. If you're a tech blogger and you read, then, please let people know. Create the impression that the tastemakers think being cultured again. Otherwise I fear that someday I'll have to sit through another insufferable office Christmas party, and I don't think I can survive that.
